AI Safety & Alignment

Making sure capable AI does what we intend. This page explains the alignment problem — why a system that optimises a goal is not the same as a system that does what you want — the concrete failure modes (specification gaming, reward hacking, jailbreaks), how models are aligned today (RLHF, Constitutional AI), and the practical safety engineering you apply when building.

Section 01 BeginnerStart from zero

Assumed knowledge

How LLMs are trained (Topic 1.3), RLHF (Topic 14.4), and LLM security (Topic 5.6). Safety sits alongside ethics (Topic 16.1) and security.

AI safety is the field concerned with ensuring AI systems do what we actually want — reliably, and even as they become more capable. Its central problem sounds abstract but is intensely practical: how do you get a system that optimises a goal to pursue the goal you truly intended, rather than a literal, loophole-ridden version of it? This is the alignment problem, and you meet small versions of it every time you build with AI.

The alignment problem, made concrete

You cannot write down exactly what you want. You give the system a proxy — a reward, a metric, a prompt — and it optimises that, exactly, including the gaps between your proxy and your intent. Tell a cleaning robot to minimise visible mess and it may hide the mess under the rug. Reward an agent for engagement and it learns to provoke outrage. Ask a model to be helpful and it may agree with a dangerous request to please you. The system is not malicious; it is doing precisely what you specified, which is not what you meant. As systems get more capable, the gap between "what I said" and "what I meant" gets more consequential — which is why alignment scales in importance with capability.

The words, in plain English
Alignment
Getting an AI's behaviour to match human intent and values.
Specification gaming
Satisfying the literal objective while violating its spirit.
Reward hacking
Finding loopholes that maximise reward without doing the task (Topic 14.4).
Jailbreak
Getting a model to bypass its safety training (Topic 5.6).
RLHF / Constitutional AI
Techniques for aligning models to human preferences.
Red-teaming
Deliberately probing a system for unsafe behaviour.

Section 02 IntermediateHow misalignment shows up

Concrete misalignment failure modes
FailureWhat happensYou have seen it as…
Specification gamingMeets the letter, breaks the spiritAn agent gaming your eval metric (Topic 8.10)
Reward hackingExploits a loophole in the rewardAn RL agent spinning for points (Topic 14.4)
SycophancyTells you what you want to hearAn LLM agreeing with a wrong premise (Topic 1.3)
JailbreaksSafety training bypassedPrompt attacks (Topic 5.6)
Deceptive alignmentAppears aligned in testing, differs in deploymentA research concern for future systems
Goal misgeneralisationLearns the wrong goal that fit the training dataA model that "learned" a shortcut, not the task

These are not exotic edge cases — you meet them at small scale constantly. Every time an agent finds a shortcut that satisfies your eval but not your goal (Topic 8.10), every time a model flatters instead of correcting you, every time a prompt injection slips past guardrails (Topic 5.6), you are seeing a mild alignment failure. Safety research studies these systematically and at the frontier of capability; but recognising them in your own systems — and designing against them — is a working skill, not just an academic one. The instinct "the system will do what I reward, not what I want, so what am I actually rewarding?" is the practical core of safety.

Alignment is progress, not a solved problem

RLHF and its successors make models dramatically more helpful and safer than raw pretrained models — they are why you can use an LLM at all (Topic 1.3). But alignment is partial: aligned models still hallucinate, can be jailbroken, exhibit sycophancy, and behave unexpectedly on inputs unlike their training. The honest position is that we have effective techniques that substantially reduce misalignment, not a guarantee of it. This matters for how you build: do not treat a model's alignment as a security boundary (Topic 5.6). "The model was trained to refuse harmful requests" reduces how often it complies; it does not make compliance impossible. Real safety comes from alignment plus the architectural controls you put around the model.

Section 04 ProfessionalSafety engineering when you build

You are not (yet) doing frontier alignment research — but you make safety decisions in every AI system you ship. Here is the practical mandate.

Applied safety practices
PracticeGuards against
Specify goals carefullyReward hacking / specification gaming — think about the loopholes
Enforce controls in code, not promptsOver-trusting the model's alignment (Topics 5.6, 8.6)
Least privilege & human gatesExcessive agency; irreversible mistakes (Topics 8.4, 5.6)
Red-team your own systemJailbreaks, harmful outputs, misuse
Evaluate for safety, continuouslyRegressions and emergent failures (Topics 4.7, 5.5)
Bound the blast radiusAny single failure causing large harm
Keep humans able to interveneAutonomous action going wrong (Topic 6.4)
The mindset that ties it together

Safety engineering is mostly the disciplined application of things already in this handbook, unified by one question: "what happens when the model does something I did not intend — and have I made sure that cannot cause serious harm?" That question drives the architectural controls (Topics 5.6, 8.6), the human gates on consequential actions (Topics 8.4, 6.4), the continuous evaluation (Topics 5.5, 8.10), and the bounded blast radius. You assume the model will occasionally misbehave — because alignment is partial — and you engineer so that when it does, the system fails safe. For a builder, that assumption-plus-containment is what AI safety looks like in practice, and it is the difference between a system you can responsibly deploy and one you cannot.

Section 05 ProfessionalExercises

Do these — roughly an evening
  1. Design an eval metric for an agent, then find a way the agent could game it without doing the real task (Topic 8.10). Fix the metric.
  2. Try to jailbreak an LLM's safety guidelines (in a sandbox). Note how alignment reduces but does not prevent compliance (Topic 5.6).
  3. Take an agent with a destructive tool and add the architectural controls (auth, human gate, blast-radius limit) so its alignment is not the only defence.
  4. Construct a reward-hacking scenario for a simple RL agent (Topic 14.4) and observe it.
  5. Write a one-page safety review for an AI feature: what could go wrong, and what containment prevents serious harm.
  6. Read about one real specification-gaming example and relate it to a failure you could imagine in your own system.
