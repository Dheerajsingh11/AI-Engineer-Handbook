Alignment & Preference Optimization

Teaching a model what “good” means. This page covers post-training alignment — RLHF, reward modelling, and the preference-optimisation methods (DPO, ORPO) that replaced it — plus GRPO, the technique behind modern reasoning models. How each one turns human preferences into model behaviour, and why SFT alone is not enough.

Section 01 BeginnerStart from zero

Assumed knowledge

Fine-tuning/SFT (Topic 3.5) and reinforcement learning basics (Topic 14.4). This page is the step after supervised fine-tuning — how a model is aligned to human preferences.

A pretrained model predicts likely text; a supervised-fine-tuned (SFT) model follows instructions in the right format. But neither knows which of two valid answers a human would actually prefer — more helpful, more honest, less harmful. Alignment (or preference optimisation) is the final training stage that teaches exactly that, and it is what turns a raw instruction-follower into an assistant people want to use.

The three-stage pipeline

Modern chat models are built in three stages. 1. Pretraining — learn language from a huge corpus (Topic 9.5). 2. SFT — learn to follow instructions from example answers (Topic 3.5). 3. Preference optimisation — learn which answers humans prefer, from comparisons. SFT can only imitate the answers it was shown; it cannot learn from “A is better than B”. Stage 3 is where a model learns to be genuinely helpful, and it is the difference between a base model and a Claude/GPT-style assistant.

The words, in plain English
Preference pair
Two answers to the same prompt, labelled which one a human preferred (chosen vs rejected).
Reward model
A model trained to score answers the way humans would.
RLHF
Reinforcement Learning from Human Feedback — the original alignment recipe.
DPO
Direct Preference Optimisation — align directly on preference pairs, no RL loop.
GRPO
Group Relative Policy Optimisation — the RL method behind modern reasoning models.

Section 02 IntermediateRLHF & reward modelling

The original method, and still the conceptual foundation. RLHF has two steps: train a reward model to imitate human judgement, then use reinforcement learning to push the language model toward high-reward outputs.

The reward model — learning human preference (Bradley–Terry) P(yw ≻ yl) = σ( r(x,yw) − r(x,yl) ) Given a prompt x and a preferred answer yw over a rejected one yl, train a reward model r so the preferred answer scores higher (σ is the sigmoid). Humans compare pairs; the reward model turns those comparisons into a scalar score for any answer.

With a reward model in hand, RLHF then uses PPO (a policy-gradient RL algorithm, Topic 14.4) to update the language model: generate answers, score them with the reward model, and nudge the model toward higher-scoring behaviour — while a KL penalty keeps it from drifting too far from the SFT model (which would let it “hack” the reward with gibberish that scores high). It works, and it produced the first great chat models — but it is complex and unstable: you train a separate reward model, run an RL loop, and juggle several models at once. That complexity is exactly what the next methods remove.

Section 03 IntermediateDPO & ORPO — the simpler way

Direct Preference Optimisation is the insight that you do not need a separate reward model or an RL loop. You can optimise the language model directly on the preference pairs with a simple classification-style loss.

The DPO loss — align directly on (chosen, rejected) pairs L = − log σ( β log π(yw|x)πref(yw|x) − β log π(yl|x)πref(yl|x) ) Raise the model's probability of the chosen answer yw and lower it for the rejected yl, relative to a frozen reference model πref (the SFT model). β controls how far it may move. No reward model, no sampling, no RL — just a loss on pairs you can train like ordinary supervised learning. This is why DPO became the default.

ORPO — folding two stages into one

ORPO (Odds-Ratio Preference Optimisation) goes further: it merges SFT and preference tuning into a single training run and needs no separate reference model. You add an odds-ratio penalty to the ordinary SFT loss that pushes down the rejected answers as you learn the chosen ones. Fewer stages, fewer models to hold in memory — attractive when compute is tight. In practice, the whole DPO/ORPO family is available in one library (Hugging Face TRL), so trying them is a config change, not a research project.

Section 04 AdvancedGRPO & training reasoning models

Group Relative Policy Optimisation is the method behind the recent leap in reasoning models (it powered DeepSeek-R1). It is an RL method, like RLHF, but stripped down for a specific setting: tasks where you can check whether an answer is correct.

Why GRPO matters for reasoning

RLHF needs a learned reward model; GRPO often does not, because for maths, code and logic you have a verifiable reward — the answer is right or wrong, the code passes tests or fails. GRPO's trick is to drop the expensive value model that normal PPO needs. Instead, for each prompt it samples a group of answers, scores them all, and computes each answer's advantage relative to the group's average — better-than-average answers are reinforced, worse-than-average ones are discouraged. No value network, cheaper and more stable. Pointed at verifiable problems with this reward, models learn to reason — to produce long chains of thought that actually reach correct answers (Topic 9.4). That is the training story behind the reasoning-model wave.

GRPO — advantage relative to the group Ai = ri − mean(r1..G)std(r1..G) For a group of G sampled answers to one prompt, each answer's reward ri is normalised against the group's mean and spread. The model is pushed toward the answers that beat their peers. Replacing PPO's learned value baseline with this simple group baseline is what makes GRPO cheap enough to scale on reasoning tasks.

Section 05 ProfessionalChoosing & in practice

Which method, when
SituationReach for
Align a chat model to human preferenceDPO (or ORPO to save a stage)
Maximum control, have the infraRLHF/PPO (what frontier labs still use)
Train a reasoning model with verifiable rewardsGRPO
Scarce human labelsRLAIF — use an AI to generate the preferences

Two practical notes. Where the labels come from is often the hard part: human preference data is expensive, so RLAIF (RL from AI feedback) and constitutional methods use a strong model to generate the preference judgements against a written set of principles — the approach behind much modern alignment (Topic 16.2). And alignment is also a safety tool: the same machinery that makes a model helpful is what makes it refuse harmful requests and stay honest, which is why this page sits next to AI Safety (Topic 16.2) as much as fine-tuning (Topic 3.5). For most engineers the takeaway is concrete: after SFT, a round of DPO on a few thousand good preference pairs is the highest-leverage, most accessible way to make a fine-tuned model noticeably better — and it is a handful of lines in TRL.

Section 06 ProfessionalExercises

Do these — align a small model
  1. Take an SFT model and a small preference dataset (e.g. UltraFeedback). Run DPO with TRL and compare answers before/after.
  2. Sweep the DPO β. Watch how a small β lets the model move far (and sometimes degrade) vs a large β keeping it close to the reference.
  3. Try ORPO on the same data in one step (no separate SFT). Compare the result and the training time to SFT-then-DPO.
  4. Build a tiny verifiable-reward task (arithmetic: reward = is-correct). Sketch how GRPO would sample a group and reinforce the winners.
  5. Write a reward-model training loop conceptually: pairs in, a scalar score out, Bradley–Terry loss. Explain why RLHF then needs a KL penalty.
  6. Explain to a colleague why SFT cannot learn “A is better than B” but DPO can. If you can, you understand this page.
