Governance, Privacy & Compliance

Section 01 BeginnerStart from zero

Assumed knowledge

How RAG stores data (Phase 4) and how memory persists (Topic 5.3). No legal background needed — this is the engineering view of the rules, not legal advice.

Governance is the least exciting topic in this roadmap and the one most likely to stop your project shipping. A model that works brilliantly is worthless if legal will not approve its launch, and legal will not approve it if you cannot answer their questions about data, decisions and deletion.

Why this is an engineering problem, not a paperwork problem

Almost every governance requirement translates into something you must build: an audit trail, a deletion path, a PII filter, a human-review step, a data-retention job. Retrofitting these into a live RAG system — especially deletion, once personal data is baked into embeddings — is painful and sometimes nearly impossible. Building them in from the start costs little. This page is about knowing what to build before someone tells you that you needed it three months ago.

None of this is legal advice. It is the engineer's map of where the requirements land in your architecture, so you can have an informed conversation with the people whose job the law actually is.

The words, in plain English
PII
Personally identifiable information — anything that identifies a person.
Data subject
The person the data is about. Under GDPR they have rights.
Right to erasure
A person can require you to delete their data ("right to be forgotten").
Data residency
Rules about which country or region data may be stored and processed in.
Automated decision-making
A decision affecting someone made without meaningful human involvement. Specially regulated.
Audit trail
An immutable record of what happened, who did it, and on what basis.
Data processing agreement (DPA)
The contract governing how a vendor may handle your data.

Section 02 IntermediateThe EU AI Act — risk tiers

The EU AI Act classifies AI systems by risk, and the tier determines your obligations. It has extraterritorial reach: if your system affects people in the EU, it applies regardless of where you are.

The four risk tiers
TierExamplesObligation
UnacceptableSocial scoring, manipulative or exploitative systemsBanned
High riskHiring, credit, education, healthcare, essential services, law enforcementConformity assessment, risk management, human oversight, logging, documentation
Limited riskChatbots, most business assistantsTransparency — tell people they are dealing with AI
Minimal riskSpam filters, most internal toolingNone specific
The tier you land in is easy to underestimate

"It's just a support chatbot" sounds like limited risk. But if that chatbot decides who gets a refund, who is escalated, or who is denied a service, it may be making decisions that touch the high-risk category — hiring and creditworthiness are explicitly listed. The obligations at high risk are substantial: documented risk management, human oversight built into the workflow, detailed logging, and a conformity assessment before deployment.

Determine your tier early, with your legal team, because a high-risk classification changes what you build. Human oversight is not a checkbox you add at the end — it is a review step in the flow, an override capability, and a logged record that a human could and did intervene.

Section 03 IntermediateGDPR for LLM systems

GDPR principles, and what each demands of your system
PrincipleWhat it means for an LLM system
Lawful basisYou need a legal reason to process personal data — consent, contract, legitimate interest
Purpose limitationData collected for support cannot be silently repurposed to train a model
Data minimisationDo not send more personal data to the model than the task needs
Right of accessA person can ask what data you hold about them — including in memory and embeddings
Right to erasureYou must be able to delete a person's data — from the database, memory, and vectors
Right to rectificationA person can correct wrong data you hold about them
Automated-decision rightsThe right not to be subject to solely automated decisions with significant effect, and to an explanation
Data protection by designPrivacy built in from the start, not added later
Sending data to a model provider is a data transfer

Every prompt you send to a hosted model is a transfer of whatever personal data that prompt contains to a third party, often in another jurisdiction. This triggers real obligations: a data processing agreement with the provider, a lawful basis for the transfer, and confirmation that the provider does not train on your data (most business tiers contractually commit to this — verify it, in writing).

Practical controls: minimise PII in prompts, redact where possible, prefer providers with EU data residency for EU data, and for the most sensitive data consider self-hosting (Topic 6.1) so nothing leaves your VPC at all. Your legal team owns the DPA; you own the data-minimisation and residency engineering.

Section 04 AdvancedHandling PII in the pipeline

JavaRedaction at the boundary, with reversible tokens
@Component
class PiiRedactionAdvisor implements CallAdvisor {

    private final PiiDetector detector;      // Presidio, or a cloud DLP service
    private final TokenVault vault;

    @Override public int getOrder() { return 0; }   // FIRST -- before anything else sees the text

    @Override
    public ChatClientResponse adviseCall(ChatClientRequest request,
                                         CallAdvisorChain chain) {

        // 1. Detect and replace PII with stable placeholders BEFORE the
        //    prompt reaches the provider. The model reasons over
        //    <PERSON_1> and <EMAIL_1>, never the real values.
        var detected = detector.scan(request.prompt().getContents());
        var redacted = detected.isEmpty()
                ? request
                : request.mutate().prompt(replaceWithTokens(request, detected)).build();

        // Store the mapping so we can restore real values in the answer
        // if policy allows -- scoped to this request only.
        var mapping = vault.store(detected, Duration.ofMinutes(5));

        ChatClientResponse response = chain.nextCall(redacted);

        // 2. Rehydrate only where the user is entitled to see the value.
        //    For many use cases you leave the tokens in place.
        return restoreAuthorised(response, mapping, currentPrincipal());
    }
}

// Detection covers the categories that matter and is never perfect --
// treat it as one layer, not a guarantee.
enum PiiCategory {
    NAME, EMAIL, PHONE, ADDRESS, NATIONAL_ID, CREDIT_CARD,
    IBAN, IP_ADDRESS, DATE_OF_BIRTH, HEALTH, BIOMETRIC
}
Redaction is a layer, not a wall

PII detectors miss things — unusual name formats, PII embedded in free text, identifiers that only become sensitive in combination. Do not treat redaction as a guarantee that no personal data reaches the provider. Combine it with data minimisation (do not retrieve fields the task does not need), a provider contract that forbids training on your data, and — for the most sensitive workloads — self-hosting. Defence in depth, exactly as in security (Topic 5.6), because the failure mode here is a compliance breach rather than an exploit.

Section 05 AdvancedThe right to erasure, when data is in embeddings

This is the requirement that catches teams out, because personal data in a RAG system is not in one place. It is in the source rows, in the chunks, in the vectors, in conversation memory, in cached responses, and possibly in provider logs. "Delete this person" means all of them.

JavaA complete deletion path
@Service
class DataErasureService {

    @Transactional
    public ErasureReport erase(String subjectId, ErasureRequest request) {
        var report = new ErasureReport(subjectId, Instant.now());

        // 1. Source records
        report.add("source", sourceRepo.deleteBySubject(subjectId));

        // 2. Chunks AND their vectors. Deleting the row without the
        //    vector, or vice versa, leaves recoverable personal data.
        var documentIds = documentRepo.findBySubject(subjectId);
        for (var docId : documentIds) {
            vectorStore.delete("document_id == '" + docId + "'");
            report.add("vectors", chunkRepo.deleteByDocument(docId));
        }

        // 3. Conversation memory -- short-term AND long-term episodes
        report.add("sessions", memoryStore.deleteByUser(subjectId));
        report.add("episodes", episodeStore.delete(
                "user_id == '" + subjectId + "'"));
        report.add("facts", factRepo.deleteByUser(subjectId));

        // 4. Caches -- a semantic cache can hold an answer about them
        report.add("cache", semanticCache.evictBySubject(subjectId));

        // 5. Provider-side: request deletion of retained logs per your DPA.
        //    You cannot do this yourself -- it is a contractual process.
        providerDataController.requestDeletion(subjectId, request.reference());

        // 6. Audit the erasure itself -- you must prove you did it,
        //    which means keeping a record ABOUT the deletion (lawful,
        //    because it is required to demonstrate compliance).
        auditLog.recordErasure(report);
        return report;
    }
}
Why deletion must be designed in, not bolted on

Two things make erasure hard if you did not plan for it. First, you must be able to find all of a person's data — which means every chunk, vector, episode and cache entry needs a subject_id attached at write time. Retrofitting that mapping onto data already ingested may be impossible, because the link between a vector and the person it concerns was never recorded.

Second, a trained or fine-tuned model that learned from personal data cannot simply forget it. This is a strong reason to keep personal data in RAG (deletable) rather than fine-tuning it into weights (not deletable) — the same RAG-versus-fine-tuning decision from Topic 3.5, now with a legal dimension. If you fine-tune on personal data, erasure may require retraining.

Section 06 ProfessionalThe audit trail

"The AI decided" is not an answer a regulator, a court, or an angry customer will accept. For any decision with real effect, you need a record of what was decided, on what basis, with what data, and whether a human was involved.

JavaA decision record built for defensibility
public record AiDecisionRecord(
        String decisionId,
        String subjectId,               // who it was about
        String decisionType,            // "refund_eligibility", "escalation"
        String outcome,

        // Reproducibility: enough to reconstruct exactly what happened
        String modelId, String modelVersion,
        String promptVersion,
        List<String> retrievedChunkIds, // ids, not text (Topic 5.5)
        List<ToolInvocation> toolCalls,

        // Explainability: the stated reasoning, with its caveats
        String rationale,
        double confidence,

        // Human oversight: the AI Act requirement made concrete
        boolean humanReviewed,
        String reviewerId,
        ReviewOutcome reviewOutcome,    // APPROVED, OVERRIDDEN, MODIFIED

        Instant timestamp,
        String lawfulBasis) {           // why this processing was permitted

    // Immutable and append-only. An audit trail you can edit is not
    // an audit trail. Write to append-only storage or sign records.
}
The governance controls, mapped to where they live in your system
RequirementWhere it is implemented
Transparency (it's AI)UI disclosure at the point of interaction
Human oversightA review step in the workflow, with override and logging
ExplainabilityRationale and citations stored on the decision record
Data minimisationRetrieval and prompt construction — fetch only what is needed
PII protectionRedaction advisor at the model boundary (Section 04)
Right to erasureThe deletion service (Section 05)
Audit trailAppend-only decision records (this section)
Bias monitoringTrack outcomes across protected groups; alert on disparity
Data residencyProvider choice and self-hosting (Topic 6.1)
RetentionScheduled jobs that expire data per policy
Bias is measurable, and measuring it is your job

A model can produce systematically different outcomes for different groups — approving refunds less often for certain names, escalating some accents more, scoring some dialects as lower quality. This is both an ethical problem and, in high-risk domains, a legal one.

You cannot fix what you do not measure. Log decision outcomes with enough (carefully handled) demographic context to compute disparity across protected groups, and alert when outcomes diverge beyond a threshold. This is the same measurement discipline as everywhere else in the roadmap (Topics 4.7, 5.5), pointed at fairness. Treat a disparity alert as a production incident, not a research finding.

Section 07 ProfessionalA pre-launch checklist

Answer these before you ship — with your legal team
  1. What EU AI Act tier is this system, and what obligations follow?
  2. What personal data does it process, and what is the lawful basis?
  3. Is there a DPA with every model provider, and do they contractually not train on our data?
  4. Can we delete all of one person's data — rows, chunks, vectors, memory, cache — and prove we did?
  5. Can we reconstruct any decision: prompt version, model version, retrieved data, tool calls, rationale?
  6. Is there human oversight on decisions with significant effect, and is intervention logged?
  7. Are users told they are interacting with AI?
  8. Do we redact or minimise PII before it reaches the provider?
  9. Is sensitive data kept in RAG (deletable) rather than fine-tuned into weights (not)?
  10. Do we monitor outcomes for bias across protected groups?
  11. Is there a data-retention policy, and a job that enforces it?
  12. Where is the data stored and processed, and does that satisfy residency requirements?
