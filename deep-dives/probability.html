Section 01 BeginnerStart from zero

Assumed knowledge

None. Fractions and percentages are enough for Sections 01–02. Logarithms appear in Section 05 and are explained where they appear.

Probability is the mathematics of not knowing. That is worth saying plainly, because it explains why it sits at the heart of AI: a language model does not know what word comes next. It has opinions, weighted by confidence. Probability is how you write those opinions down.

The one rule everything is built on

A probability is a number between 0 and 1. Zero means impossible, one means certain, and the probabilities of all possible outcomes must add up to exactly 1.

That last clause does more work than it looks. It means probability is a budget. If you become more confident about one outcome, you must take that confidence from somewhere else. When you later see temperature "sharpening" a distribution, this is what is happening: the budget is being redistributed, never created.

The words, in plain English
Random variable
A thing whose value you do not know yet. The next token, tomorrow's request count, whether this email is spam.
Distribution
The full list of possible values and how likely each one is. "60% cat, 30% dog, 10% bird" is a distribution.
Conditional probability
The probability of something given that you already know something else. Written P(A|B), read "probability of A given B". Knowing it rained changes your estimate of whether the ground is wet.
Prior / posterior
What you believed before seeing evidence, and what you believe after. Updating one into the other is the whole of Bayesian reasoning.
Logits
Raw, unbounded scores a model produces before they are converted into probabilities. They can be negative, or 40, or anything. They are not probabilities yet.
Softmax
The recipe that turns logits into a proper distribution — all positive, adding to 1.
Sampling
Actually picking one outcome according to the probabilities. Roll the weighted die.

What an LLM is really doing

Here is the entire generation loop, stripped of jargon. You give the model "The capital of France is". It produces a score for every single token in its vocabulary — perhaps 100,000 of them. Softmax turns those scores into probabilities:

What the model actually returns for "The capital of France is ___"
TokenLogitProbability
Paris12.40.891
the9.80.066
located8.90.027
a7.60.007
…99,996 more—0.009

Then it picks one, appends it to the text, and does the whole thing again. That is all. There is no lookup, no database, no fact-checking step. A hallucination is what happens when the highest-probability continuation is not the true one — the machine is working exactly as designed.

Why this framing pays off immediately

Once you see the model as a distribution rather than an oracle, three mysteries dissolve at once. Temperature is a knob on that table. Hallucination is a wrong table, not a bug. Evaluation must be statistical, because the output is a sample rather than a value. Everything in Phase 5 about evals follows from this one shift.

Section 02 BeginnerConditional probability and Bayes

Most useful probability is conditional. Not "is this email spam?" but "is this email spam given that it contains the word 'invoice'?"

Bayes' theorem is the rule for flipping a conditional round. It is easy to state and famously easy to misapply, so it is worth deriving rather than memorising.

Deriving Bayes' theorem in three lines
  1. P(A ∩ B) = P(A|B) · P(B) Definition of conditional probability: the chance both happen equals the chance B happens, times the chance A happens given it did.
  2. P(A ∩ B) = P(B|A) · P(A) The same quantity, decomposed the other way round. "Both happened" does not care which you condition on.
  3. P(A|B) = P(B|A) · P(A) / P(B)   ■ Set (1) equal to (2) and divide by P(B). That is the whole theorem.

Why it matters: the base rate trap

A worked example that has caught out doctors, lawyers and — relevantly — people building classifiers. A test for a disease is 99% accurate. One person in 10,000 has the disease. You test positive. What is the chance you have it?

Applying Bayes P(disease | positive) = 0.99 × 0.0001(0.99 × 0.0001) + (0.01 × 0.9999) = 0.0098 Under 1%. The intuition says 99%; the answer is 1%. The reason is that false positives are drawn from the enormous healthy population — 100 false alarms for every true detection. Rare things stay rare even after positive evidence. This is exactly why a fraud classifier with 99% accuracy on a 0.1% fraud rate is worthless, and why Section 07 insists you never report accuracy alone.

Section 03 IntermediateDistributions worth recognising

A distribution is a shape of uncertainty. You need to recognise about seven of them on sight; the rest you can look up.

The two numbers that summarise any distribution

Expectation E[X] = Σ xipi is the long-run average — each outcome weighted by how often it happens. Variance E[(X−μ)2] is the average squared distance from that average, so it measures spread. In practice you will report a mean latency and a p95: the mean is expectation, and the gap to p95 is variance made concrete. Reporting a mean without a spread is the most common way engineers mislead themselves with numbers.

Section 04 IntermediateSoftmax and temperature, derived

You have a vector of logits. You need a probability distribution. Three requirements: every output positive, all outputs summing to 1, and larger logits mapping to larger probabilities. Softmax is the standard answer — but why that formula?

  1. Make everything positive. Logits can be negative, so you need a function that is always positive. exp(x) is the natural choice: always positive, monotonically increasing, and smooth everywhere so gradients exist.
  2. Force the sum to 1. Divide each exponentiated value by the total of all of them. Now they are a valid distribution by construction.
  3. Notice the side effect. Exponentiating exaggerates differences. A logit 2 points ahead becomes roughly 7× more likely, not 2×. Softmax is deliberately opinionated.
Softmax with temperature P(i)  =  exp(zi / T)Σj exp(zj / T) Temperature divides the logits before exponentiating, which rescales all the gaps between them at once. Small T stretches the gaps apart, so the leader runs away with the probability mass. Large T squashes the gaps together, so everything approaches equally likely. The name comes from statistical physics, where the same equation (the Boltzmann distribution) describes how particles occupy energy states at temperature T.

The two limits, and why they behave that way

  • T → 0. Dividing by a tiny number magnifies every gap enormously. The largest logit's exponential dwarfs all others, its probability goes to 1, and sampling becomes argmax. This is greedy decoding.
  • T → ∞. Every zi/T approaches 0, every exp approaches 1, and all outcomes become equally likely. Pure noise.
Numerical stability — the bug everyone writes once

exp(800) overflows to infinity in float64, and inf/inf is NaN. Since softmax is unchanged if you subtract a constant from every logit (the constant factors out of numerator and denominator and cancels), always subtract the maximum logit first. Then the largest exponent is exp(0) = 1 and nothing can overflow. Every production implementation does this; if you write your own and skip it, you will get NaN on your first real batch.

Section 05 AdvancedBuild it from scratch

Sampling strategies, implemented

PythonEvery decoding strategy, from nothing
import numpy as np

def softmax(logits, T=1.0):
    z = np.asarray(logits, dtype=np.float64) / T
    z = z - z.max()                   # stability: largest exponent becomes 0
    e = np.exp(z)
    return e / e.sum()

def greedy(logits):
    return int(np.argmax(logits))     # deterministic, repetitive

def sample_temperature(logits, T=0.7, rng=None):
    rng = rng or np.random.default_rng()
    return int(rng.choice(len(logits), p=softmax(logits, T)))

def sample_top_k(logits, k=40, T=1.0, rng=None):
    rng = rng or np.random.default_rng()
    logits = np.asarray(logits, dtype=np.float64)
    keep = np.argpartition(-logits, k)[:k]        # k largest, unsorted
    p = softmax(logits[keep], T)                  # renormalise over survivors
    return int(keep[rng.choice(len(keep), p=p)])

def sample_top_p(logits, p_thresh=0.9, T=1.0, rng=None):
    """Nucleus sampling: smallest set of tokens whose mass reaches p."""
    rng = rng or np.random.default_rng()
    probs = softmax(logits, T)
    order = np.argsort(-probs)                    # descending
    cumulative = np.cumsum(probs[order])
    # +1 so we always keep at least one token, and always cross the threshold
    cutoff = int(np.searchsorted(cumulative, p_thresh)) + 1
    keep = order[:cutoff]
    renorm = probs[keep] / probs[keep].sum()
    return int(keep[rng.choice(len(keep), p=renorm)])

def sample_min_p(logits, min_p=0.05, T=1.0, rng=None):
    """Keep tokens at least min_p as likely as the best token."""
    rng = rng or np.random.default_rng()
    probs = softmax(logits, T)
    keep = np.flatnonzero(probs >= min_p * probs.max())
    renorm = probs[keep] / probs[keep].sum()
    return int(keep[rng.choice(len(keep), p=renorm)])

def apply_repetition_penalty(logits, generated, penalty=1.1):
    """Divide positive logits of seen tokens; multiply negative ones."""
    logits = np.array(logits, dtype=np.float64)
    for t in set(generated):
        logits[t] = logits[t] / penalty if logits[t] > 0 else logits[t] * penalty
    return logits

# See how the distribution changes shape
logits = [12.4, 9.8, 8.9, 7.6, 3.1, 1.0]
for T in (0.2, 0.7, 1.0, 1.5):
    print(f"T={T}: {np.round(softmax(logits, T), 4)}")

# T=0.2: [1.     0.     0.     0.     0.     0.    ]  -- effectively greedy
# T=0.7: [0.969  0.024  0.007  0.001  0.     0.    ]
# T=1.0: [0.899  0.067  0.027  0.007  0.     0.    ]
# T=1.5: [0.759  0.134  0.074  0.031  0.002  0.    ]  -- more adventurous

A complete classifier from scratch: Naive Bayes

Bayes' theorem is not just theory — it is a working classifier that was the state of the art for spam filtering for a decade, trains in one pass, and still beats neural networks when you have 200 labelled examples. Building it makes the theorem concrete.

The "naive" part is an assumption: that words appear independently of each other given the class. This is obviously false — "New" and "York" are not independent — but the classifier works well anyway, because it only needs the ranking of class scores to be right, not the probabilities themselves.

PythonNaive Bayes in 40 lines, no libraries
import math, re
from collections import defaultdict, Counter

class NaiveBayes:
    def __init__(self, alpha=1.0):
        self.alpha = alpha            # Laplace smoothing strength
        self.log_prior = {}
        self.log_likelihood = {}
        self.vocab = set()

    @staticmethod
    def tokens(text):
        return re.findall(r"[a-z']+", text.lower())

    def fit(self, docs, labels):
        n = len(docs)
        by_class = defaultdict(list)
        for d, y in zip(docs, labels):
            by_class[y].extend(self.tokens(d))
        self.vocab = {w for words in by_class.values() for w in words}
        V = len(self.vocab)

        class_counts = Counter(labels)
        for c, words in by_class.items():
            # P(class) -- how common is this class at all?
            self.log_prior[c] = math.log(class_counts[c] / n)

            # P(word | class), with +alpha so an unseen word
            # does not zero out the entire product.
            counts = Counter(words)
            total = len(words) + self.alpha * V
            self.log_likelihood[c] = {
                w: math.log((counts[w] + self.alpha) / total) for w in self.vocab
            }
            self.log_likelihood[c]["<UNK>"] = math.log(self.alpha / total)

    def predict(self, doc):
        # Work in log space: products of many small numbers underflow
        # to zero in floating point, but sums of logs are stable.
        scores = {}
        for c in self.log_prior:
            s = self.log_prior[c]
            for w in self.tokens(doc):
                s += self.log_likelihood[c].get(w, self.log_likelihood[c]["<UNK>"])
            scores[c] = s
        return max(scores, key=scores.get), scores

docs = ["free money now", "win a free prize", "meeting at three",
        "project meeting tomorrow", "claim your free reward", "lunch tomorrow?"]
labels = ["spam", "spam", "ham", "ham", "spam", "ham"]

nb = NaiveBayes()
nb.fit(docs, labels)
print(nb.predict("free lunch tomorrow")[0])   # ham -- 'lunch'+'tomorrow' outweigh 'free'
print(nb.predict("claim free money")[0])      # spam
Two techniques worth stealing from this code

Log space. Multiplying 500 probabilities each around 0.001 underflows to exactly 0.0 in float64, destroying the answer. Adding their logarithms does not. Any time you multiply many small probabilities — language model scoring, HMMs, beam search — work in logs. This is also why models report log probabilities.

Smoothing. Without +alpha, one unseen word makes P(document|class) exactly zero, vetoing the class no matter how much other evidence supports it. Adding a pseudo-count to everything means "I have never seen this, but it is not impossible". The same idea reappears as regularisation in Section 2.3.

Section 06 AdvancedInformation theory: how models are scored

Four quantities, all variations on "how surprised were you?". They are how language models are trained, compared and monitored, so it is worth being precise about the differences.

The family Entropy H(p) = −Σi pi log pi Cross-entropy H(p,q) = −Σi pi log qi KL divergence DKL(p‖q) = H(p,q) − H(p) Perplexity PPL = exp(H(p,q)) Read them as a sequence. Entropy is your average surprise if reality matches your beliefs. Cross-entropy is your average surprise when reality is p but you believed q — always at least as large. KL is the excess: the price of being wrong. Perplexity exponentiates cross-entropy back into "effective number of equally likely choices", which is the only one of the four that is human-readable.

Why the logarithm? Because information should add. Learning two independent facts should give you twice the information, but their probabilities multiply. The logarithm is the function that turns multiplication into addition, so −log p is the unique sensible measure of surprise. Base 2 gives bits; base e gives nats, which is what deep learning frameworks use.

PythonScoring a language model the way training does
import numpy as np

def cross_entropy(true_ids, predicted_probs, eps=1e-12):
    """Mean negative log-probability the model assigned to what
    actually came next. This IS the LLM training loss."""
    p = np.clip(predicted_probs[np.arange(len(true_ids)), true_ids], eps, 1.0)
    return float(-np.log(p).mean())

def perplexity(true_ids, predicted_probs):
    return float(np.exp(cross_entropy(true_ids, predicted_probs)))

def entropy(p, eps=1e-12):
    """Uncertainty of a single prediction -- a cheap hallucination signal."""
    p = np.clip(np.asarray(p, dtype=float), eps, 1.0)
    return float(-(p * np.log(p)).sum())

# A confident prediction vs an uncertain one
confident = np.array([0.95, 0.03, 0.01, 0.01])
uncertain = np.array([0.30, 0.28, 0.22, 0.20])
print(f"confident H = {entropy(confident):.3f} nats")   # 0.246
print(f"uncertain H = {entropy(uncertain):.3f} nats")   # 1.371

# In production: log per-token entropy alongside the answer.
# A spike in mean entropy across a response correlates with
# the model making things up, and costs nothing to compute.

Section 07 ProfessionalEvaluation metrics, and why accuracy lies

Every classification result is one of four things. Lay them out and every metric becomes obvious.

The confusion matrix — the source of all classification metrics
Predicted positivePredicted negative
Actually positiveTrue Positive (TP) — correct hitFalse Negative (FN) — a miss
Actually negativeFalse Positive (FP) — a false alarmTrue Negative (TN) — correct rejection

Now the definitions read themselves. Precision = TP/(TP+FP) looks down the "predicted positive" column: when I raise the alarm, am I right? Recall = TP/(TP+FN) looks across the "actually positive" row: of everything I should have caught, how much did I catch?

Why accuracy is the metric that lies

Accuracy is (TP+TN)/total — it counts true negatives, which are usually abundant and worthless. On a fraud dataset with 0.1% fraud, a model that predicts "never fraud" scores 99.9% accuracy while catching zero fraud. It is a perfect score for a useless model, and it is the single most common way an ML project reports success while delivering nothing. Report precision and recall, or PR-AUC, and accuracy will stop deceiving you.

The trade-off is real and it is yours to set

Precision and recall pull against each other. Lower your decision threshold and you catch more true cases (recall up) but raise more false alarms (precision down). There is no universally correct point on that curve — it depends on the relative cost of the two errors, which is a business decision, not a mathematical one.

Fβ — encoding which error you fear more Fβ  =  (1 + β2) · precision · recall(β2 · precision) + recall β=1 gives the familiar F1, weighting both equally. β=2 weights recall four times as heavily — use it when a miss is far worse than a false alarm, as in disease screening or security scanning. β=0.5 does the reverse. Note it is a harmonic mean, so unlike an ordinary average it cannot be gamed by making one of the two very high while the other collapses.
PythonEvery metric from scratch, then verified against scikit-learn
import numpy as np

def confusion(y_true, y_pred, positive=1):
    y_true, y_pred = np.asarray(y_true), np.asarray(y_pred)
    tp = int(((y_pred == positive) & (y_true == positive)).sum())
    fp = int(((y_pred == positive) & (y_true != positive)).sum())
    fn = int(((y_pred != positive) & (y_true == positive)).sum())
    tn = int(((y_pred != positive) & (y_true != positive)).sum())
    return tp, fp, fn, tn

def metrics(y_true, y_pred, beta=1.0):
    tp, fp, fn, tn = confusion(y_true, y_pred)
    precision = tp / (tp + fp) if tp + fp else 0.0
    recall    = tp / (tp + fn) if tp + fn else 0.0
    b2 = beta * beta
    denom = b2 * precision + recall
    fbeta = (1 + b2) * precision * recall / denom if denom else 0.0
    return {
        "accuracy":  (tp + tn) / max(tp + fp + fn + tn, 1),
        "precision": precision,
        "recall":    recall,
        f"f{beta:g}": fbeta,
        "specificity": tn / (tn + fp) if tn + fp else 0.0,
    }

def average_precision(y_true, scores):
    """Area under the precision-recall curve -- the honest metric
    for imbalanced data. Walk the threshold down, accumulate."""
    order = np.argsort(-np.asarray(scores))
    y = np.asarray(y_true)[order]
    tp = np.cumsum(y)
    precision = tp / np.arange(1, len(y) + 1)
    total_pos = y.sum()
    return float((precision * y).sum() / total_pos) if total_pos else 0.0

# The imbalanced case that exposes accuracy
rng = np.random.default_rng(0)
y_true = np.zeros(10_000, dtype=int); y_true[:10] = 1   # 0.1% positive
never_fires = np.zeros(10_000, dtype=int)

print(metrics(y_true, never_fires))
# {'accuracy': 0.999, 'precision': 0.0, 'recall': 0.0, 'f1': 0.0, ...}
# 99.9% accurate. Catches nothing. Precision and recall tell the truth.

Averaging across classes, and metrics for ranking

Multi-class averaging — state which one you used
MethodHowEffect
MacroCompute per class, then average unweightedA tiny class counts as much as a huge one. Use when rare classes matter
MicroPool all TP/FP/FN, then compute onceDominated by frequent classes; equals accuracy in single-label problems
WeightedPer class, averaged by class frequencyA compromise; can hide poor performance on rare classes

RAG is a ranking problem, not a classification one, so it needs order-aware metrics. These return in §4.7, but the definitions belong here:

  • Precision@k / Recall@k — the same formulas, restricted to the top k results.
  • MRR — mean of 1/(rank of first correct result). Rewards getting one right answer to the top. Use when there is a single correct document.
  • NDCG@k — discounted cumulative gain, normalised. Handles graded relevance and penalises correct results that appear far down the list. The most informative and the least intuitive.
Statistical significance — the professional habit

Prompt A scores 82% on 50 test cases; prompt B scores 86%. Is B better? With n=50, the 95% confidence interval on 82% is roughly ±11 points. The intervals overlap heavily, so you have learned nothing. A rough rule: the standard error of a proportion is √(p(1−p)/n), so detecting a 5-point difference reliably needs several hundred cases, not fifty. Report intervals alongside every eval number, and treat differences smaller than the interval as noise. This one habit will save you from shipping prompt changes that do nothing.

Section 08 ProfessionalExercises

Do these — roughly one evening
  1. Implement softmax without the max-subtraction trick and feed it [800, 799]. Observe the NaN. Then add the trick and watch it work. You will never forget it afterwards.
  2. Print the softmax of a fixed logit vector at T = 0.1, 0.5, 1.0, 2.0, 5.0. Plot the five distributions on one chart. This picture is what "temperature" means.
  3. Implement top-p sampling. Then check: at p=0.9, how many tokens survive for a peaked distribution versus a flat one? That adaptivity is the whole argument for nucleus over top-k.
  4. Build the Naive Bayes classifier on the SMS Spam Collection dataset. Report precision, recall and F1 — not accuracy. Then deliberately report accuracy too, and note how much better it makes a mediocre model look.
  5. Work the medical-test Bayes calculation for prevalences of 1 in 100, 1 in 1,000 and 1 in 100,000. Plot posterior probability against prevalence. This is the base-rate fallacy made visual.
  6. Take any eval you have run and compute a 95% confidence interval on the pass rate. Decide honestly whether your last "improvement" was inside the noise.
