Section 01 BeginnerStart from zero

Assumed knowledge

The handbook, broadly. This page is about converting what you have learned into a role — the last mile of the transition.

Knowing the material is necessary but not sufficient. Getting an AI-engineering role requires demonstrating that knowledge in two settings: a portfolio that proves you can ship, and interviews that test whether you can reason about systems. This page covers both, plus the honest positioning that turns a backend background into an advantage rather than a gap.

What actually gets you hired (from Topic 9.1 recap)

The 2026 job market screens for evidence, not vocabulary. Every candidate lists "RAG", "LLMs", "agents" — those words are now table stakes and prove nothing. What gets callbacks is shipped, quantified work: "built a RAG system over 50k documents, improved answer accuracy from 60% to 85% measured on a 200-case eval set, deployed to 500 users." The difference between the two is the entire difference between an interview and a rejection. Everything below serves one goal: producing and communicating that kind of concrete evidence.

The capstone projects from the hub are your portfolio — a semantic-search tool, an evaluation harness, a production RAG service, an event-driven agent, and (given a security or domain background) something that shows your differentiator. Build them publicly, and treat the README as the deliverable: it should explain the problem, your design decisions and their trade-offs, the evaluation and the numbers, and what you would do next. Hiring managers read READMEs; a project with a thoughtful write-up beats a cleverer project with none. Quality over quantity — three deep, deployed, measured projects beat ten notebooks.

Section 03 IntermediateThe AI system-design interview

Senior AI-engineering interviews increasingly include a system-design round: "design a RAG system for a company's documents," "design an AI customer-support agent." This is where the whole handbook pays off — you have built these systems, so you can reason about them. A framework for answering.

  1. Clarify requirements. Scale (documents, users, QPS)? Latency budget? Accuracy bar? Cost constraints? Data sensitivity? Do not start designing until you know these — asking shows seniority.
  2. Sketch the high-level architecture. Ingestion pipeline, retrieval, generation, serving — the shape from Phase 4, drawn out.
  3. Go deep on the hard parts. Chunking strategy, hybrid search + re-ranking (Topic 4.6), evaluation (Topic 4.7), the parts that actually determine quality.
  4. Address the cross-cutting concerns. Cost (routing, caching), latency (streaming), safety (Topic 5.6), evaluation and monitoring (Topic 5.5), failure modes.
  5. Discuss trade-offs. Every choice has alternatives — managed vs custom, open vs closed model, RAG vs fine-tuning. Naming the trade-off is what they are testing.
  6. Know the failure modes. Hallucination, poor retrieval, cost blow-ups, prompt injection — and how you would catch and handle each.
What the interviewer is really assessing

They are not looking for one right answer — there isn't one. They are assessing whether you reason like someone who has built these systems: do you ask about requirements before designing, do you know that retrieval quality (not the model) usually determines RAG quality, do you bring up evaluation unprompted, do you name trade-offs instead of asserting one true way, do you know the failure modes? Every one of these instincts comes directly from having worked through this handbook — especially the "measure it" and "fix retrieval first" and "controls in code, not prompts" themes. The system-design interview is where reading pays off in a way that reciting definitions never could.

Section 04 AdvancedPositioning a transition

If you are moving into AI from another engineering background, positioning that background correctly is the difference between "junior AI engineer" and "engineer who adds AI to real systems."

Your background is an asset, not a deficit

The market is full of people who know how to call an LLM API but have never run a production system, handled scale, thought about security, or shipped something reliable. If you come from backend, data, security, or a specific domain, you already have the half of AI engineering that is hardest to teach — distributed systems, deployment, observability, data modelling, security, domain knowledge. The AI-specific skills (this handbook) are the faster half to acquire. So do not position yourself as a beginner competing with fresh ML grads; position yourself as an experienced engineer who now also builds AI systems. "I've run production services for years and I've built RAG and agent systems on top of that experience" is a far stronger story than "I'm learning AI." Lead with the combination.

The habits that decide whether this works (a recap of the whole handbook's ethos): build in public — a GitHub repo per project with a real README is your actual CV. Measure everything — the evaluation discipline (Topics 4.7, 5.5) is what separates engineers from demo-builders, and it is rarer than it should be. Ship, don't just study — a deployed project teaches and proves more than any number of tutorials. Stay current — the field moves monthly; keep a maintenance loop (Topic 11.3). And lead with what you already have — you are not starting over, you are adding a layer to real experience. That framing, more than any single skill, is what makes the transition succeed.

A final word

You have reached the end of the knowledge base. The distance from here to a role is not more reading — it is doing: building the projects, measuring them, deploying them, writing them up, and telling the story of an experienced engineer who now builds AI. The material is complete; the differentiator is execution. Everything in these pages was chosen to make you effective, not just informed — so go build something real, measure it honestly, and ship it. That is the whole job.

Section 06 ProfessionalExercises

Do these — your actual job search
  1. Build and deploy the capstone projects. Write a strong README for each: problem, design, trade-offs, evaluation, numbers.
  2. Do a mock AI system-design interview (design a RAG system, or a support agent) using the Section 03 framework. Record yourself; review it.
  3. Write your positioning statement: one paragraph framing you as an experienced engineer who builds AI, leading with your background (Section 04).
  4. Build the one project only you would build — your background plus AI. Make it your portfolio centrepiece.
  5. Rewrite your CV to show shipped, quantified AI work — evidence, not vocabulary (Section 01).
  6. Pick three real job descriptions. For each, map which handbook topics it requires and where your background is the edge.
