18.3

Model Compression & Distillation

Making models small enough to actually ship. This page covers the three ways to shrink a model — knowledge distillation, pruning and quantisation — how a big "teacher" trains a small "student", why a smaller model is often the right engineering choice, and how these techniques produced the small, fast models running today.

Section 01 BeginnerStart from zero

Assumed knowledge

Neural network basics (Topic 2.4) and the quantisation intro from serving (Topic 6.1). This page goes deeper and adds the two techniques that page did not cover.

The best model is often too big: too slow to serve, too expensive to run, too large to fit on a phone or a GPU you can afford. Model compression is the set of techniques that make a model smaller and faster while keeping most of its accuracy — turning a research artefact into something you can actually deploy.

Why smaller is often better engineering

A model half the size that keeps 97% of the accuracy is usually the better product: it costs less per call, responds faster, fits on more hardware, and can even run on-device (Topic 19.4). Frontier capability grabs headlines, but most shipped AI runs on compressed models — distilled, pruned, quantised versions of larger ones. Knowing how to make a model small is as valuable a skill as knowing how to make one accurate, and often more so once something has to run at scale.

The three techniques, in plain English
Quantisation
Store the numbers with fewer bits (32 → 8 → 4). Smaller, faster, slightly less precise.
Pruning
Delete the weights that barely matter, leaving a sparser network.
Knowledge distillation
Train a small "student" model to imitate a big "teacher" model.
Teacher / student
The large, accurate model and the small, fast one learning from it.

Section 02 IntermediateKnowledge distillation

The most powerful of the three, and the one behind most small models worth using. Instead of training a small model from scratch on the raw labels, you train it to imitate a large, already-trained model.

The insight is that a teacher's full output carries more information than a hard label. When a good model sees a photo of a dog, it does not just say "dog" — it says "92% dog, 7% wolf, 1% cat." Those soft targets reveal how the teacher sees similarity between classes, and a student trained to match them learns faster and generalises better than one trained only on the one-hot answer "dog." The student soaks up the teacher's reasoning about the data, not just its final verdict.

Distillation loss — imitate the teacher, respect the labels L = α · Lsoft( student, teacher@T ) + (1 − α) · Lhard( student, true label ) The student matches the teacher's softened probabilities (temperature T > 1 spreads them out to expose the "dark knowledge" in the small numbers) and the true labels. α balances the two. Set T = 1, α = 0 and you are back to ordinary training.
PythonThe distillation loss, in essence
import torch.nn.functional as F

def distill_loss(student_logits, teacher_logits, labels, T=3.0, alpha=0.5):
    # Soft: match the teacher's softened distribution (KL divergence).
    soft = F.kl_div(
        F.log_softmax(student_logits / T, dim=-1),
        F.softmax(teacher_logits / T, dim=-1),
        reduction="batchmean") * (T * T)          # T^2 keeps gradients scaled
    # Hard: still learn from the real labels.
    hard = F.cross_entropy(student_logits, labels)
    return alpha * soft + (1 - alpha) * hard
You already use distilled models

DistilBERT (40% smaller, ~97% of BERT's performance, 60% faster) is a distilled model. Most small open models are distilled from larger ones, and the frontier labs distil their big models into the fast, cheap tiers you call by API. When a provider offers a "mini" or "flash" model, distillation is usually how it was made. This is not a lab-only technique — it is how production AI gets affordable.

Section 03 IntermediatePruning & quantisation

The other two techniques shrink a given model rather than training a new small one, and they compose — you can distil, then prune, then quantise.

Pruning and quantisation compared
PruningQuantisation
IdeaRemove weights near zeroStore weights in fewer bits
GrainUnstructured (individual weights) or structured (whole neurons/heads)fp16, int8, int4 — whole-tensor or per-group
Speed gainReal only if structured (hardware likes dense blocks)Large — less memory moved, faster maths
When appliedDuring or after training, then fine-tune to recoverPost-training (PTQ) or quantisation-aware (QAT)
LLM relevanceModerate — harder on huge modelsEssential — how big models fit on real GPUs

Quantisation is the one you will use most. As Topic 6.1 showed, moving from 16-bit to 4-bit weights roughly quarters the memory and speeds up inference, for a small, often negligible accuracy cost — which is why nearly every locally-run LLM (Topic 9.2) is quantised (GGUF, GPTQ, AWQ). Pruning is more situational: unstructured pruning makes a model sparse but not necessarily faster, because ordinary hardware still processes the zeros; structured pruning removes whole units and gives real speedups but is harder to do without hurting accuracy. Distillation, meanwhile, is the one that can actually improve a small model rather than merely shrink a big one.

The mental model

Think of it as three independent knobs. Distillation changes which model you have — a new, smaller architecture trained to think like the big one. Pruning removes parts of the network you have. Quantisation changes how precisely each remaining number is stored. They stack: a strong production pipeline might distil a 70B teacher into a 7B student, prune redundant heads, then quantise to 4-bit — ending with something 20× cheaper to serve that a user cannot distinguish from the original on the task that matters. The art is spending accuracy only where it buys you real efficiency.

Section 05 ProfessionalIn production

The professional discipline is to measure the trade you are actually making. Compression is never free — it costs some accuracy — so the only question that matters is whether the accuracy you lose is worth the cost, latency and memory you gain, on your task. Benchmark the compressed model against the original on a real evaluation set (Topic 19.1), not a generic one; a model can hold up on MMLU and quietly degrade on your niche. Watch for uneven damage: quantisation often hits the rare, long-tail cases hardest while leaving the average looking fine. And prefer, when you can, to let someone else pay the cost — a well-made pre-distilled, pre-quantised model off the shelf is usually a better starting point than compressing one yourself.

Section 06 ProfessionalExercises

Do these — roughly a weekend
  1. Distil a small classifier from a larger one on a dataset you know. Compare it to the same small model trained on labels alone.
  2. Sweep the temperature T and mixing weight α in the distillation loss. See how each changes the student.
  3. Quantise an open LLM to int8 and int4 (bitsandbytes or GGUF). Measure size, speed and quality on a real task.
  4. Prune a CNN's weights by magnitude at 50% and 90%. Compare unstructured vs structured for actual latency, not just sparsity.
  5. Compose the techniques: distil, then quantise. Confirm the gains stack and measure the combined accuracy cost.
  6. Take a task where a "mini" API model underperforms. Decide honestly whether distillation would beat just prompting the big one.
