Section 01 BeginnerStart from zero

Assumed knowledge

None. If you can add and multiply numbers, you can read this section. School algebra helps from Section 03 onward, but nothing here requires you to have seen a matrix before.

Forget vectors and matrices for a moment. Start with a problem you already understand.

Suppose you run a bookshop and you want to recommend books. You could describe every book by three numbers: how romantic it is, how violent it is, and how funny it is — each scored from 0 to 10.

Three books, described by three numbers each
BookRomanceViolenceHumour
A quiet love story912
A gentle romance803
A war thriller191

Look at the rows. The first two books have similar numbers, and they are also similar books. The third has very different numbers, and it is a very different book. You did not need any mathematics to notice that — but notice what just happened: you compared meanings by comparing numbers.

That list of three numbers, [9, 1, 2], is a vector. That is the entire definition. A vector is an ordered list of numbers describing something.

The words, in plain English
Vector
An ordered list of numbers. [9, 1, 2]. "Ordered" matters — the first slot always means romance, the second always violence. Swapping them would change the meaning.
Dimension
How many numbers are in the list. Our books are 3-dimensional. Real text embeddings are typically 768 or 1536-dimensional — the same idea, just with far more descriptive slots, and nobody chose what each slot means.
Embedding
A vector that a machine learning model produced to describe a piece of text. The word "embedding" just means "we embedded this text into a space of numbers".
Matrix
A grid of numbers — effectively a stack of vectors. The whole table above, without the labels, is a 3×3 matrix.
Dot product
A recipe for turning two vectors into a single number that says how alike they are. Multiply the matching slots together, then add up the results.
Norm
The length of a vector — how "big" the list of numbers is overall.
Cosine similarity
The dot product, adjusted so that length does not matter and only direction counts. Gives 1 for identical, 0 for unrelated, −1 for opposite.

Doing it by hand, once

Let us compare "A quiet love story" [9, 1, 2] with "A gentle romance" [8, 0, 3]. Multiply the matching slots, then add:

A dot product, worked out in full (9 × 8)  +  (1 × 0)  +  (2 × 3) =   72  +  0  +  6   =   78 Now the war thriller [1, 9, 1] against the same love story: (9×1) + (1×9) + (2×1) = 9 + 9 + 2 = 20. The similar pair scored 78; the dissimilar pair scored 20. That is the entire mechanism behind semantic search, and you just performed it with arithmetic a ten-year-old could do.

Two things are worth noticing before you go further, because they are the seeds of everything that follows.

  • Big numbers in the same slot make the score big. Both books score high on romance, so 9×8 = 72 dominates the total. Agreement in a slot is rewarded; disagreement contributes almost nothing, because one of the two numbers is small.
  • Long books would cheat. If a book scored [90, 10, 20] — the same proportions, ten times bigger — its dot product with everything would be ten times larger, and it would win every comparison regardless of subject. Fixing that unfairness is exactly what cosine similarity does, and why it, rather than the raw dot product, is the default in search.
The whole page in one paragraph

Text becomes lists of numbers. Similar text becomes similar lists. Comparing two lists is multiply-and-add. Everything else on this page — the proofs, the code, the strange behaviour at 1536 dimensions — is detail hanging off that sentence. If you only take one thing from Phase 1, take that.

What changes in the real thing

Three differences separate the bookshop example from a production embedding:

  1. Nobody labels the slots. We chose "romance, violence, humour". A real model learns its own 1536 slots from data, and no individual slot has a name a human would recognise. This is fine — the geometry works regardless.
  2. The numbers come from a model, not a person. You feed text in, a neural network produces the vector. Section 06 of this page builds a simple version of that model so you can see there is no magic in it.
  3. There are a lot more slots. Three dimensions behave the way your intuition expects. Fifteen hundred do not, in ways that genuinely matter for engineering — which is what Section 07 is about.

Section 02 BeginnerThe idea behind it

Computers cannot compare meanings. They can compare numbers. The whole of modern natural language processing rests on one bridge between those two facts, and it is worth stating precisely because everything else follows from it.

The bridge is the distributional hypothesis, proposed by the linguist J.R. Firth in 1957: a word is characterised by the company it keeps. If two words appear in similar contexts, they mean similar things. "Doctor" and "physician" both appear near "patient", "hospital", "diagnosis". "Doctor" and "bicycle" do not.

That is a testable, countable claim. And once you can count it, you can put it in a vector.

The move that makes everything work

Represent each word as a list of numbers describing its context. Words used similarly get similar lists. Now "is X similar to Y?" — a question about meaning, which computers cannot answer — becomes "are these two lists of numbers pointing in the same direction?", which is arithmetic. Semantics has been converted into geometry.

Linear algebra is the study of that geometry. That is the only reason it appears in this roadmap, and it is why you need the specific parts covered here and not a whole degree's worth.

What a vector actually is

You will meet three definitions, and they describe the same object from different angles. Holding all three at once is what "having intuition for linear algebra" means.

Three views of a vector
ViewA vector is…Useful when
Computer scienceAn ordered list of numbers. float[1536]Writing code, reasoning about memory and cost
Physics / geometryAn arrow with direction and length, from the originReasoning about similarity, angles, projection
MathematicsAn element of a space closed under addition and scalingUnderstanding why the operations are the ones they are

The mathematical view is the one people skip, and it is the one that explains the rules. A vector space is any set where you can add two elements and scale an element by a number, and the results stay in the set. That is nearly the whole definition. It is why you can average embeddings and get something meaningful, and why every operation in a neural network is built from exactly those two moves.

Section 03 IntermediateThe dot product, derived

Almost every textbook states the dot product twice and never connects the two statements:

The two definitions a · b  =  a1b1 + a2b2 + … + anbn   (algebraic) a · b  =  ‖a‖ ‖b‖ cos θ   (geometric) One is a sum of products of coordinates. The other is about lengths and an angle. That these are the same number is not obvious, and the proof is the single most clarifying thing in elementary linear algebra.

Here is why they are equal. Take the triangle formed by a, b and the vector a−b joining their tips, and apply the law of cosines — the generalisation of Pythagoras to non-right triangles.

Deriving the geometric form from the algebraic one
  1. ‖a−b‖2 = ‖a‖2 + ‖b‖2 − 2‖a‖‖b‖ cos θ Law of cosines applied to the triangle. Purely geometric — no coordinates yet.
  2. ‖a−b‖2 = Σi (ai − bi)2 Now expand the same quantity in coordinates, using the definition of length.
  3. = Σi ai2 − 2Σi aibi + Σi bi2 Multiply out the square, term by term.
  4. = ‖a‖2 − 2Σi aibi + ‖b‖2 The first and third sums are exactly the squared lengths.
  5. ‖a‖2 + ‖b‖2 − 2‖a‖‖b‖cos θ  =  ‖a‖2 − 2Σi aibi + ‖b‖2 Set line 1 equal to line 4 — both describe ‖a−b‖2.
  6. ‖a‖‖b‖ cos θ  =  Σi aibi   ■ Cancel the squared lengths, divide by −2. The two definitions are the same number.

Why this matters practically. The algebraic form is what your CPU computes — a multiply-accumulate loop, the single most optimised operation in computing. The geometric form is what it means — alignment of direction. Line 6 says you get the semantic quantity for free from the cheap arithmetic one. Every vector database on earth is an industrial exploitation of that equality.

Reading the sign

  • Large positive — same direction, and both vectors are long. Strong agreement.
  • Zero — perpendicular, orthogonal. In embedding space this means "unrelated".
  • Negative — opposed directions. Rarer in practice than beginners expect, because most embedding models place unrelated things at roughly 90°, not 180°.

Section 04 IntermediateNorms, normalisation, and cosine

A norm answers "how long is this vector?". The definition you know is one of an infinite family, all instances of the p-norm:

The p-norm family ‖a‖p  =  ( Σi |ai|p )1/p p=1 gives Manhattan distance (sum of absolute values); p=2 gives ordinary Euclidean length; as p→∞ only the largest component survives, giving the max norm. The choice of p decides which errors a model is punished for — L1 tolerates a few large deviations and pushes small ones to exactly zero, which is why Lasso regression produces sparse models.

Normalising divides a vector by its own L2 norm, producing a vector of length exactly 1 pointing the same way. All information about magnitude is discarded; all information about direction is kept.

Why cosine similarity is the dot product of normalised vectors
  1. cos θ = (a · b) / (‖a‖‖b‖) Rearranged directly from the geometric definition.
  2. Let â = a/‖a‖ and b̂ = b/‖b‖, so ‖â‖ = ‖b̂‖ = 1 Normalise both.
  3. â · b̂ = (a · b) / (‖a‖‖b‖) = cos θ Scalars pull straight out of a dot product. So on normalised vectors, dot product is cosine similarity — the division is already done.
The engineering consequence

Normalise once, at write time, and every subsequent query is a bare dot product: n multiplies and n adds, no square roots, no division. Over a hundred million stored vectors queried thousands of times per second, that is a substantial saving for zero loss of accuracy. This is why every vector database documentation page tells you to normalise on ingest and query with inner product.

The relationship to Euclidean distance

There is one more identity worth knowing, because it explains why the metric choice sometimes does not matter at all. For normalised vectors:

Squared Euclidean distance on the unit sphere ‖â − b̂‖2  =  2 − 2(â · b̂) Expand the square and use ‖â‖2 = ‖b̂‖2 = 1. Distance is a decreasing linear function of similarity, so ranking by Euclidean distance gives exactly the same order as ranking by cosine — provided the vectors are normalised. If they are not, the two metrics genuinely disagree, and that is where silent retrieval bugs come from.

Section 05 IntermediateMatrices as functions

The standard presentation — "a matrix is a grid of numbers, and here is the rule for multiplying two of them" — makes matrix multiplication look arbitrary. It is not arbitrary. It is forced.

A matrix is a linear transformation: a function that takes a vector and returns a vector, obeying two rules — T(u+v) = T(u)+T(v) and T(cv) = cT(v). Rotation, scaling, projection and shear all qualify.

Here is the key fact: a linear transformation is completely determined by what it does to the basis vectors. Write v = v1e1 + v2e2, apply linearity, and T(v) = v1T(e1) + v2T(e2). So if you store T(e1) and T(e2) as the columns of a grid, you can reconstruct T for any input.

The one sentence to remember

The columns of a matrix are the images of the basis vectors. That is what a matrix is: a lookup table of where the axes land. Everything else — the multiplication rule, why the inner dimensions must match, why AB ≠ BA — follows from this without further invention.

Deriving the multiplication rule

If A and B are both transformations, AB should mean "do B, then do A" — function composition. Work out where the basis vectors land under that composition and the familiar formula falls out with no freedom of choice:

Composition forces the multiplication rule
  1. (AB)(ej) = A(B(ej)) Definition of composition, applied to basis vector j.
  2. B(ej) = column j of B, whose entries are Bkj By the boxed fact above.
  3. = Σk Bkj ek Write that column back out in the basis.
  4. A(Σk Bkj ek) = Σk Bkj A(ek) Linearity of A — this is the only property used.
  5. = Σk Bkj Σi Aik ei = Σi (Σk AikBkj) ei Expand A(ek) and swap the order of summation.
  6. (AB)ij = Σk AikBkj   ■ The bracketed quantity is entry i of column j — the rule you were taught, now inevitable rather than arbitrary.

Two consequences drop out immediately. The index k must range over the same set on both sides, so the inner dimensions must match. And "rotate then stretch" is a different function from "stretch then rotate", so matrix multiplication does not commute.

Section 06 AdvancedBuild it from scratch

Implement the primitives with no libraries first. This takes twenty minutes and permanently removes the sense that NumPy is doing something mysterious.

PythonStep 1 — the primitives, pure Python
import math

def dot(a, b):
    if len(a) != len(b):
        raise ValueError(f"dimension mismatch: {len(a)} vs {len(b)}")
    total = 0.0
    for x, y in zip(a, b):
        total += x * y                # multiply-accumulate: the whole operation
    return total

def norm(a, p=2):
    if p == 1:  return sum(abs(x) for x in a)
    if p == 2:  return math.sqrt(sum(x * x for x in a))
    return max(abs(x) for x in a)     # p = infinity

def normalize(a):
    n = norm(a)
    if n == 0:
        return list(a)                # zero vector has no direction
    return [x / n for x in a]

def cosine(a, b):
    denom = norm(a) * norm(b)
    return 0.0 if denom == 0 else dot(a, b) / denom

def matmul(A, B):
    n_rows, n_inner, n_cols = len(A), len(B), len(B[0])
    if len(A[0]) != n_inner:
        raise ValueError("inner dimensions must match")
    # Exactly line (6) of the derivation above.
    return [[sum(A[i][k] * B[k][j] for k in range(n_inner))
             for j in range(n_cols)]
            for i in range(n_rows)]

# Verify the identity we proved: cosine == dot on normalised vectors
a, b = [3.0, 4.0, 0.0], [1.0, 2.0, 2.0]
assert abs(cosine(a, b) - dot(normalize(a), normalize(b))) < 1e-12
print(f"cos = {cosine(a, b):.4f}, angle = {math.degrees(math.acos(cosine(a,b))):.1f} deg")

Step 2 — build a real embedding space from raw text

This is the part almost nobody does, and it is the part that makes embeddings click. You are going to turn the distributional hypothesis into an actual vector space, with no neural network involved. The recipe predates deep learning and still works.

  1. Count co-occurrences. For every word, count how often each other word appears within a window of ±k positions. This gives a vocabulary×vocabulary matrix of raw counts.
  2. Reweight. Raw counts are dominated by "the" and "of". Positive Pointwise Mutual Information asks whether two words co-occur more than chance would predict, which is the actual signal.
  3. Reduce. The matrix is huge and mostly zeros. Truncated SVD compresses it to a few hundred dense dimensions, and — crucially — forces words with similar contexts to collapse onto similar vectors.
  4. Normalise and search. You now have embeddings. Cosine similarity between them is semantic similarity.
PythonStep 2 — embeddings from a corpus, no neural network
import numpy as np, re
from collections import Counter, defaultdict

def tokenize(text):
    return re.findall(r"[a-z']+", text.lower())

def build_embeddings(corpus, window=4, dims=64, min_count=3):
    tokens = tokenize(corpus)

    # --- vocabulary -------------------------------------------------
    counts = Counter(tokens)
    vocab  = [w for w, c in counts.most_common() if c >= min_count]
    index  = {w: i for i, w in enumerate(vocab)}
    V      = len(vocab)

    # --- 1. co-occurrence counts ------------------------------------
    C = np.zeros((V, V), dtype=np.float64)
    for pos, word in enumerate(tokens):
        i = index.get(word)
        if i is None:
            continue
        lo, hi = max(0, pos - window), min(len(tokens), pos + window + 1)
        for other in tokens[lo:pos] + tokens[pos + 1:hi]:
            j = index.get(other)
            if j is not None:
                C[i, j] += 1.0

    # --- 2. positive pointwise mutual information -------------------
    #   PMI(i,j) = log[ P(i,j) / (P(i)P(j)) ]
    #   "do these co-occur more than independence predicts?"
    total    = C.sum()
    p_ij     = C / total
    p_i      = C.sum(axis=1, keepdims=True) / total
    p_j      = C.sum(axis=0, keepdims=True) / total
    with np.errstate(divide="ignore", invalid="ignore"):
        pmi = np.log(p_ij / (p_i * p_j))
    ppmi = np.nan_to_num(pmi, nan=0.0, neginf=0.0, posinf=0.0)
    ppmi[ppmi < 0] = 0.0              # keep only positive association

    # --- 3. truncated SVD: V dims -> `dims` dims --------------------
    #   U holds left singular vectors; scaling by sqrt(S) is the
    #   standard choice and behaves better than U or U@S alone.
    U, S, _ = np.linalg.svd(ppmi, full_matrices=False)
    emb = U[:, :dims] * np.sqrt(S[:dims])

    # --- 4. normalise so dot product == cosine ----------------------
    emb /= np.linalg.norm(emb, axis=1, keepdims=True) + 1e-9
    return emb, vocab, index

def nearest(word, emb, vocab, index, k=5):
    i = index[word]
    scores = emb @ emb[i]             # one matmul == every comparison
    scores[i] = -np.inf               # exclude the word itself
    top = np.argpartition(-scores, k)[:k]
    top = top[np.argsort(-scores[top])]
    return [(vocab[j], round(float(scores[j]), 3)) for j in top]

# Feed it any large plain-text file -- Project Gutenberg works well.
# text = open("book.txt", encoding="utf-8").read()
# emb, vocab, index = build_embeddings(text)
# print(nearest("king", emb, vocab, index))
#   -> [('queen', 0.71), ('prince', 0.68), ('throne', 0.64), ...]
What you have just proved to yourself

Nothing in that code understands language. It counted words near other words, asked which pairings beat chance, and compressed the result. Yet the output places "king" near "queen" and "throne". The geometry emerged from the counting. Modern embedding models are enormously better at this — they use transformers, contrastive training and vastly more data — but they are doing a more sophisticated version of the same thing, and the vector space they produce is used in exactly the way you just used yours.

Step 3 — PCA by hand, via power iteration

SVD above was a library call. Here is how the underlying machinery actually works, using an algorithm simple enough to write in ten lines: power iteration. It finds the direction of greatest variance by repeatedly applying the matrix and renormalising.

Why it works: write any starting vector as a combination of the eigenvectors. Each application of A multiplies each component by its eigenvalue. The largest eigenvalue grows fastest, so after enough iterations it dominates everything else and the vector aligns with its eigenvector. Renormalising each round stops the numbers exploding.

PythonStep 3 — PCA from scratch
import numpy as np

def power_iteration(A, iters=500, tol=1e-10, seed=0):
    """Dominant eigenvector of a symmetric matrix A."""
    rng = np.random.default_rng(seed)
    v = rng.normal(size=A.shape[0])
    v /= np.linalg.norm(v)
    for _ in range(iters):
        w = A @ v                       # stretch along eigen-directions
        n = np.linalg.norm(w)
        if n == 0:
            break
        w /= n                          # renormalise to stop overflow
        if np.linalg.norm(w - v) < tol: # converged
            v = w
            break
        v = w
    eigenvalue = float(v @ A @ v)       # Rayleigh quotient
    return v, eigenvalue

def pca(X, k=2):
    """Project X (n_samples, n_features) onto its top-k directions."""
    X = np.asarray(X, dtype=float)
    Xc = X - X.mean(axis=0)             # 1. centre -- PCA is about variance
    C  = (Xc.T @ Xc) / (len(Xc) - 1)    # 2. covariance matrix

    components, variances = [], []
    for _ in range(k):
        v, lam = power_iteration(C)
        components.append(v)
        variances.append(lam)
        # 3. DEFLATION: subtract this direction so the next
        #    iteration finds the largest of what remains.
        C = C - lam * np.outer(v, v)

    W = np.array(components)            # (k, n_features)
    return Xc @ W.T, W, np.array(variances)

# Sanity check against NumPy's eigendecomposition
rng = np.random.default_rng(1)
X = rng.normal(size=(400, 6)) @ rng.normal(size=(6, 6))
proj, W, var = pca(X, k=3)
reference = np.sort(np.linalg.eigvalsh(np.cov(X, rowvar=False)))[::-1][:3]
print(np.allclose(var, reference, atol=1e-6))   # True

Where this shows up in your work. Deflation — find the dominant direction, subtract it, repeat — is the same idea as Maximal Marginal Relevance in retrieval, where you take the best result, discount everything similar to it, and take the next. And the fact that a few directions carry most of the variance is exactly what makes Matryoshka embeddings possible: train so the important directions come first, and truncation becomes cheap.

Section 07 AdvancedWhy high dimensions are strange

Your geometric intuition comes from three dimensions and is actively misleading at 1536. Three specific effects matter, and you should see them numerically rather than take them on faith.

What changes as dimension grows
EffectWhat happensConsequence for you
Near-orthogonalityTwo random vectors have cosine ≈ 0 with variance 1/nAlmost everything is "unrelated" by default, so a similarity of 0.3 can already be meaningful. Absolute thresholds do not transfer between models
Distance concentrationThe ratio of farthest to nearest neighbour distance approaches 1Nearest-neighbour search becomes less discriminating; exact search loses its advantage over approximate
Volume in the shellNearly all the volume of a high-dimensional ball sits near its surfaceNormalised embeddings genuinely do live on a thin shell; intuitions about "clusters filling a region" fail
PythonSee it for yourself
import numpy as np
rng = np.random.default_rng(0)

print(f"{'dims':>6} {'mean|cos|':>10} {'near/far':>10}")
for d in (3, 10, 100, 768, 1536):
    A = rng.normal(size=(2000, d))
    A /= np.linalg.norm(A, axis=1, keepdims=True)

    # 1. how aligned are random pairs?
    cos = A[:1000] @ A[1000:].T
    mean_abs = np.abs(cos).mean()

    # 2. do near and far neighbours still differ?
    q = A[0]
    dists = np.linalg.norm(A[1:] - q, axis=1)
    ratio = dists.min() / dists.max()

    print(f"{d:>6} {mean_abs:>10.4f} {ratio:>10.4f}")

# dims   mean|cos|   near/far
#    3      0.5001     0.0384    <- geometry behaves as you expect
#   10      0.2522     0.2338
#  100      0.0798     0.5316
#  768      0.0288     0.7856    <- nearest is 79% as far as farthest
# 1536      0.0204     0.8352    <- distances have nearly collapsed

That last column is the entire justification for Phase 4's approximate indexes. When the nearest neighbour is already 84% as far away as the farthest, insisting on the mathematically exact answer buys you very little — and costs a full scan. Trading 1% recall for a hundredfold speedup is not a compromise; it is the correct engineering decision, and now you can defend it with numbers.

Section 08 ProfessionalIn production

Numerical and performance concerns
ConcernWhat to doWhy
Precisionfloat32 for storage; float64 only for accumulation if summing millions of termsHalves memory and doubles throughput at no measurable recall cost. Embedding models emit fp32 anyway
Quantisationint8 for a 4× saving, binary for 32× as a first-pass filterBinary + Hamming as a coarse filter, then exact rescoring on the survivors, is a standard two-stage design
BatchingOne (n, d) @ (d,) matmul, never a Python loopBLAS uses SIMD, cache blocking and multiple cores. Expect 50–200× over a loop
Zero vectorsGuard before dividingEmpty or whitespace-only chunks produce zero embeddings and NaN similarities that poison a whole result set
Catastrophic cancellationPrefer math.fsum or pairwise summation for very long vectorsNaive left-to-right summation of 10k floats loses precision; NumPy already uses pairwise summation
Storage sizingbytes ≈ n × d × 4, then add the index10M chunks × 1536 dims × 4 bytes = 61 GB before HNSW roughly doubles it. Size this before choosing a model
The three bugs you will actually write
  • Comparing vectors from different models. Two embedding spaces are unrelated coordinate systems. Cosine between them returns a number, and the number is meaningless. Store the model ID beside every vector and assert on it.
  • Forgetting to normalise, then using inner product. Retrieval silently starts favouring long documents, because their vectors are longer. Nothing errors. Quality just quietly degrades.
  • Transposing the wrong operand. A @ B.T and A.T @ B both run without complaint when the matrix is square, and produce entirely different answers. Assert on .shape at every boundary.

Section 09 ProfessionalExercises

Do these — roughly one evening
  1. Implement dot, norm, cosine and matmul in pure Python. Verify cosine(a,b) == dot(normalize(a), normalize(b)) to within floating-point tolerance.
  2. Time your pure-Python matmul against numpy.matmul on 200×200 matrices. Record the ratio. This number is why nobody writes the loop.
  3. Run the co-occurrence embedding builder on a public-domain book. Inspect the nearest neighbours of ten words. Find one that is wrong and work out why — usually a window that is too wide, or too little data.
  4. Try the analogy king - man + woman and find its nearest neighbour. It will probably not give queen on a small corpus. Understanding why not teaches more than a success would.
  5. Implement pca with power iteration and check your eigenvalues against numpy.linalg.eigvalsh.
  6. Reproduce the dimensionality table. Then plot recall against ef_search for an HNSW index on the same data when you reach §4.3 — the two results explain each other.
