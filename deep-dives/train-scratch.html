Train Your Own Model From Scratch

Building a language model from nothing. This page walks the full pipeline — data, tokenizer, architecture, pre-training loop, then SFT — using nanoGPT-scale code you can actually run, explains what each stage costs and why you almost never do this at frontier scale, and gives an honest map of when training from scratch is the right call versus fine-tuning.

Section 01 BeginnerStart from zero

Assumed knowledge

Transformer mechanics (Topic 1.3), the training loop (Topic 2.5), and fine-tuning (Topic 3.5). This page is the whole model lifecycle, from raw text to a working model.

You asked how to train your own local AI model. There are two very different things people mean by that, and separating them is the first lesson:

Two meanings of "train your own model"
Fine-tuning (Topic 3.5)Pre-training from scratch (this page)
Start fromAn existing trained modelRandom weights — nothing
TeachesStyle, format, a taskLanguage and knowledge, from zero
Data neededHundreds to thousands of examplesBillions to trillions of tokens
CostDollars to hundredsThousands to millions
You will do it…OftenAlmost never at scale — but once, small, to understand it
Why build a tiny one anyway

You will essentially never pre-train a frontier model — it costs millions and needs a data-centre. But building a small one (a few million parameters, on a laptop) is one of the most clarifying things you can do in this whole roadmap. Every abstraction — tokens, attention, the training loop, loss going down, sampling — becomes concrete when you have watched a model you built learn to produce text from nothing. That is the goal of this page: understanding through construction, not a production model.

Section 03 AdvancedBuild and train a small GPT

The whole pipeline, at a scale that runs on modest hardware. This is nanoGPT territory — a few hundred lines total; here is the shape.

PythonData and tokenizer
import numpy as np

# --- STAGE 1-2: text -> tokens. For a tiny model, a character-level
# "tokenizer" is enough to learn from and keeps things simple.
text = open("input.txt", encoding="utf-8").read()   # e.g. all of Shakespeare
chars = sorted(set(text))
stoi = {c: i for i, c in enumerate(chars)}
itos = {i: c for c, i in stoi.items()}
encode = lambda s: [stoi[c] for c in s]
decode = lambda ids: "".join(itos[i] for i in ids)

data = np.array(encode(text), dtype=np.int64)
n = int(0.9 * len(data))
train_data, val_data = data[:n], data[n:]           # honest held-out split

def get_batch(split, block_size=256, batch_size=64):
    d = train_data if split == "train" else val_data
    ix = np.random.randint(len(d) - block_size, size=batch_size)
    x = np.stack([d[i:i+block_size] for i in ix])
    y = np.stack([d[i+1:i+block_size+1] for i in ix])  # y is x shifted by one
    return x, y   # the model predicts the NEXT token at every position
PythonThe pre-training loop (PyTorch)
import torch, torch.nn as nn
from torch.nn import functional as F

# --- STAGE 3: a small transformer (the block from Topic 1.3) -------
model = GPT(vocab_size=len(chars), n_layer=6, n_head=6,
            n_embd=384, block_size=256)              # ~10M params
model = model.to(device)

# --- STAGE 4: PRE-TRAINING = next-token prediction, at scale -------
# This IS how GPT is trained. The only differences at frontier
# scale are: bigger model, more data, more GPUs, longer. The loop
# is the same one you built in Topic 2.5.
optim = torch.optim.AdamW(model.parameters(), lr=3e-4)

for step in range(5000):
    x, y = get_batch("train")
    x, y = torch.tensor(x, device=device), torch.tensor(y, device=device)

    logits = model(x)                                # (batch, seq, vocab)
    # Cross-entropy over the whole sequence (Topic 1.2). This single
    # loss, minimised over enough data, is where language ability
    # and knowledge come from.
    loss = F.cross_entropy(logits.view(-1, logits.size(-1)), y.view(-1))

    optim.zero_grad(set_to_none=True)
    loss.backward()
    torch.nn.utils.clip_grad_norm_(model.parameters(), 1.0)
    optim.step()

    if step % 500 == 0:
        print(f"step {step}: loss {loss.item():.3f}")   # watch it fall

# --- Generate: the base model can now continue text ---------------
context = torch.zeros((1, 1), dtype=torch.long, device=device)
print(decode(model.generate(context, max_new_tokens=500)[0].tolist()))
You have now seen the whole thing

That loop, run to convergence on a small corpus, produces a model that generates text in the style of its training data from a standing start. It is genuinely the same procedure as training GPT — next-token prediction, cross-entropy loss, AdamW, gradient clipping. The gap to a frontier model is scale and engineering (more parameters, trillions of tokens, thousands of GPUs, months, and enormous data and infrastructure work), not a different idea. Understanding that the idea is simple, and the scale is what is hard, is the whole point.

Section 04 AdvancedFrom base model to assistant

Your pre-trained model continues text; it does not follow instructions. Turning it into an assistant is post-training (Topic 1.3):

  1. Supervised fine-tuning (SFT). Train on instruction–response pairs so it answers rather than continues. This is the same fine-tuning machinery as Topic 3.5.
  2. Preference alignment. RLHF or DPO on human comparisons, to shape helpfulness, tone and refusals.

At small scale you can do SFT on your own model with the same PyTorch/TRL tools from Topics 3.5 and 9.3. Full preference alignment is more involved, but the concept — reward the responses humans prefer — is what you read about in Topic 1.3. The lesson: a usable assistant is pre-training for capability, then post-training for behaviour. Most of the compute is stage 4; most of the "personality" is stage 5.

Section 05 ProfessionalWhen to actually train from scratch

The honest decision
SituationDo this
You want a model that knows your factsRAG (Phase 4) — not training
You want a model that follows your format/styleFine-tune an open model (Topic 3.5)
You want a smaller/cheaper model for one taskFine-tune or distil an open model
You need a genuinely new language or modality with no good open modelContinued pre-training on an open base, maybe
You are a research lab with a data-centre and a novel architecturePre-train from scratch
You want to understand how models workBuild a tiny one (Section 03) — do this
Pre-training from scratch is almost never the right production answer

For essentially every business problem, an existing open or closed model — used directly, with RAG, or fine-tuned — is faster, cheaper and better than training your own from nothing. Pre-training a competitive model requires trillions of curated tokens, a large GPU cluster, months of work, and deep expertise in data and distributed training. The people who do it are foundation-model labs. Build the tiny one to learn; reach for RAG and fine-tuning to ship. Confusing "I trained a model" (fine-tuning) with "I built a model" (pre-training) in an interview is a tell — know the difference.

Section 06 ProfessionalExercises

Do these — roughly a weekend
  1. Clone nanoGPT and train the character-level Shakespeare model on your machine. Watch the loss fall and read the samples at each stage.
  2. Print samples at step 0, 500, 2000 and 5000. Watch it go from noise to structured text.
  3. Change one hyperparameter (layers, embedding size, learning rate) and observe the effect on loss and output quality.
  4. Swap the character tokenizer for a small BPE tokenizer (Topic 1.3). Compare sample quality at the same parameter count.
  5. Compute how long and how much it would cost to scale your setup to a 1B-parameter model on rented GPUs. Appreciate the gap.
  6. SFT your tiny base model on a handful of instruction pairs and see it start to follow instructions.
  7. Write one paragraph explaining, to a non-engineer, why training from scratch is rarely the right business choice.
