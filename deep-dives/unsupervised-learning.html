Unsupervised Learning & Clustering

Finding structure in unlabelled data. This page covers clustering (k-means, DBSCAN, hierarchical) and dimensionality reduction (PCA, t-SNE, UMAP), how to tell whether a clustering is any good without labels, and where these methods quietly power your RAG pipeline — from vector indexes to deduplication to embedding visualisation.

Section 01 BeginnerStart from zero

Assumed knowledge

Classical ML (Topic 2.3) — especially k-means — and vectors (Topic 1.1). This page deepens the unsupervised side.

Supervised learning needs labelled examples. But most data has no labels — you have a pile of customers, documents or transactions and no "answer" attached. Unsupervised learning finds structure in that raw data: groups that belong together, and the few dimensions that actually matter.

The two main unsupervised jobs
JobQuestionMethods
ClusteringWhich things belong together?k-means, DBSCAN, hierarchical
Dimensionality reductionWhat are the few dimensions that matter?PCA, t-SNE, UMAP
Why this matters for you specifically

Unsupervised learning is not a side topic for an AI engineer — it is inside your RAG pipeline. k-means is the algorithm behind IVF vector indexes (Topic 4.3). Clustering deduplicates near-identical chunks (Topic 4.5) and groups documents for routing. Dimensionality reduction visualises embedding spaces to debug them (Topic 4.2), and is the theory behind Matryoshka embeddings. These "classical" methods are load-bearing in modern LLM systems — you use them constantly, often without noticing.

The words, in plain English
Cluster
A group of data points that are similar to each other.
Centroid
The centre of a cluster — the average of its members.
Dimensionality reduction
Compressing many features into a few, keeping what matters.
Density
How tightly packed points are — the basis of DBSCAN.
Silhouette score
A label-free measure of how good a clustering is.
Embedding projection
Squashing high-dim vectors to 2D so you can see them.

Section 02 IntermediateThe clustering algorithms

When to use which clustering method
MethodHowStrengthWeakness
k-meansPartition into k round clustersFast, simple, scalable (Topic 2.3)You must pick k; assumes round clusters
DBSCANGroup dense regions; mark sparse points as noiseFinds any shape; no k; flags outliersStruggles with varying density
HierarchicalBuild a tree of nested clustersNo k up front; the dendrogram is informativeSlow on large data
Gaussian MixtureSoft, probabilistic assignmentA point can partly belong to several clustersAssumes Gaussian blobs

DBSCAN's superpower is noise. Unlike k-means, which forces every point into a cluster, DBSCAN labels sparse, isolated points as noise — which makes it a natural outlier detector (Topic 14.7) and a good deduplicator (dense clumps of near-identical items become one cluster). It also finds clusters of any shape, where k-means only finds round ones. The trade-off: it assumes clusters have similar density, and picking its distance threshold takes care. A useful rule: k-means when you know roughly how many groups and they are blob-like; DBSCAN when you do not know the count, the shapes are irregular, or outliers matter.

Section 03 IntermediateDimensionality reduction

High-dimensional data — a 1536-dim embedding, hundreds of features — is hard to visualise, sometimes noisy, and expensive to store. Reduction compresses it to a few meaningful dimensions.

The reduction methods, and a critical distinction
MethodPreservesUse for
PCAGlobal variance; linearCompression, denoising, speed (Topic 1.1)
t-SNELocal neighbourhoods; non-linearVisualising clusters in 2D
UMAPLocal + some global; fastVisualising, and preprocessing
Never cluster on t-SNE or UMAP output

t-SNE and UMAP are for looking, not for computing. They deliberately distort distances to make clusters visually separable in 2D — the gaps and cluster sizes in a t-SNE plot are not meaningful, and points far apart in the plot may be close in reality. Use them to eyeball an embedding space (Topic 4.2) and spot structure, then cluster and measure distances on the original vectors (or a PCA reduction, which does preserve distances). Treating a t-SNE layout as real geometry is a common and misleading mistake.

PythonVisualising an embedding space
import umap
from sklearn.cluster import KMeans

# You have 5,000 document embeddings (1536-dim) from Topic 4.2.
# Cluster on the FULL vectors -- distances are real there.
labels = KMeans(n_clusters=8).fit_predict(embeddings)

# Project to 2D ONLY to look at it. Do not cluster on this.
coords = umap.UMAP(n_neighbors=15).fit_transform(embeddings)
# scatter(coords, color=labels) -- see whether topics separate,
# spot outliers, sanity-check your embedding model.

Section 04 AdvancedHow do you know a clustering is good?

Without labels, "correct" has no fixed meaning — which makes evaluation genuinely hard. You have two kinds of check.

Judging a clustering
ApproachHow
Internal metricsSilhouette score, Davies-Bouldin — measure how tight and separated clusters are, no labels needed
Choosing kElbow plot (inertia vs k) or silhouette across k
StabilityDo clusters persist across re-runs and subsamples?
Human judgementDo the clusters mean something to a domain expert?
The honest truth about clustering

Internal metrics help, but the decisive test is usually "are these clusters useful and interpretable to someone who knows the domain?" A clustering with a great silhouette score that carves customers into groups nobody can act on is worthless; one with a mediocre score that reveals "these are your at-risk customers" is gold. Unsupervised results are a starting point for human insight, not a final answer. Always look at what is in each cluster — sample its members — before trusting it. This is different from supervised learning, where a held-out metric is more nearly the final word.

Section 05 ProfessionalExercises

Do these — roughly a weekend
  1. Cluster a dataset with k-means, DBSCAN and hierarchical. Compare what each finds, especially on non-round shapes.
  2. Use an elbow plot and silhouette score to choose k for k-means. Do they agree?
  3. Take document embeddings (Topic 4.2), cluster on the full vectors, then visualise with UMAP coloured by cluster. Confirm topics separate.
  4. Demonstrate the t-SNE trap: cluster on t-SNE output vs the original vectors and show the results differ misleadingly.
  5. Use DBSCAN to deduplicate near-identical items and to flag outliers in one pass.
  6. Sample the members of each cluster and write a one-line label for each. Judge whether the clustering is actually useful.
