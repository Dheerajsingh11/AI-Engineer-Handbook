Embedding Models

The component that decides your ceiling. This page explains how embedding models are trained with contrastive loss, why bi-encoders and cross-encoders exist as separate things, what Matryoshka representation actually does to the vector, and how to choose and evaluate a model on your own corpus rather than a leaderboard.

Section 01 BeginnerStart from zero

Assumed knowledge

Cosine similarity and normalisation from Topic 1.1. Section 03 refers to encoder architectures from Topic 1.3.

An embedding model is a function: text goes in, a fixed-length list of numbers comes out. Its one job is that similar text produces similar numbers.

Everything downstream depends on it. If your embedding model puts "how do I cancel my subscription" far away from the passage that explains cancellation, no amount of clever re-ranking, prompting or chunking will recover that document. The embedding model sets the ceiling on your retrieval quality.

The words, in plain English
Dimension
How many numbers in the output vector. 384, 768 and 1536 are common. More is not automatically better.
Bi-encoder
Embeds query and document separately, so documents can be indexed in advance. Fast, scalable.
Cross-encoder
Reads query and document together and scores the pair. Much more accurate, cannot be pre-indexed.
Pooling
How per-token vectors are combined into one vector for the whole text. Usually mean or CLS.
Contrastive learning
Training by pulling matching pairs together and pushing non-matching pairs apart.
Asymmetric search
Short query, long document. Needs a model trained for it — usually with prefixes.
MTEB
Massive Text Embedding Benchmark. The standard public leaderboard.
Matryoshka
An embedding trained so its leading dimensions are usable alone, allowing truncation.

Section 02 IntermediateHow embedding models are trained

A base encoder like BERT produces token vectors that are not good sentence embeddings — averaging BERT's outputs performs barely better than averaging word vectors. Something else has to happen, and that something is contrastive training.

InfoNCE / multiple-negatives ranking loss L  =  −log exp(sim(q, d+) / τ)Σj exp(sim(q, dj) / τ) Read it as a softmax classification over the batch: given query q, which of these documents is the right one? The numerator is the correct pair, the denominator sums over the correct one plus every negative. Minimising this pushes the correct pair's similarity up relative to all the others. The temperature τ (typically 0.02–0.05) controls how harshly near-misses are punished.
Why batch size matters unusually much here

With a batch of B pairs, every other document in the batch serves as a negative for each query — so you get B−1 free negatives per example with no additional labelling. A batch of 1,024 gives 1,023 negatives; a batch of 16 gives 15. This is why embedding models are trained with very large batches and gradient-cache tricks, and why fine-tuning one on a small GPU underperforms unless you use those tricks.

Hard negatives matter even more. A random negative is trivially distinguishable and teaches nothing. A hard negative — a passage that looks relevant but is not — is where the learning happens. Production training pipelines mine these by retrieving with a weaker model and taking high-scoring wrong answers.

Pooling strategies
StrategyHowUsed by
Mean poolingAverage token vectors, masked to exclude paddingMost sentence-transformers models
CLS poolingTake the special first token's vectorBERT-derived models, bge
Last-token poolingTake the final tokenDecoder-based embedding models
Weighted meanWeight later tokens more heavilySome decoder-based models
Two bugs that silently degrade every embedding you produce

Pooling over padding. If you average token vectors without applying the attention mask, padding tokens dilute every short text. Longer texts are affected less, so short queries suffer most — precisely the thing you care about. Always mask.

Missing the required prefix. E5, BGE and several other families are trained with asymmetric prefixes: query: for questions and passage: for documents. Omit them and retrieval quality drops measurably, with no error and no warning. Read the model card, every time.

Bi-encoder versus cross-encoder

This distinction determines your entire retrieval architecture, so it is worth being precise.

Two ways to score relevance
Bi-encoderCross-encoder
InputQuery and document, separatelyQuery and document, concatenated
OutputTwo vectors; you compute similarityA single relevance score
Pre-indexable?Yes — embed documents once, offlineNo — one model pass per candidate
Cost for 1M documentsOne vector search, milliseconds1M model passes. Impossible
AccuracyGoodSubstantially better
WhyQuery and document never interact before scoringAttention runs across both, so terms can match directly
The architecture this dictates

Use both, in sequence. A bi-encoder retrieves 50–100 candidates from millions in milliseconds; a cross-encoder then re-scores only those candidates to pick the final 5. You pay the expensive model's cost 100 times instead of a million times, and you get most of its accuracy. This retrieve-then-rerank pattern is the single highest-return structure in production RAG, and it is developed in §4.6.

Section 04 AdvancedMatryoshka embeddings

Normally, changing embedding dimension means changing model and re-embedding everything. Matryoshka Representation Learning removes that constraint by training so that prefixes of the vector are themselves valid embeddings.

The mechanism is straightforward once stated: during training, the contrastive loss is computed at several truncation lengths simultaneously — at 64, 128, 256, 512 and full dimension — and the losses are summed. The model is therefore forced to pack the most important information into the earliest dimensions, exactly as PCA orders components by variance (Topic 1.1).

PythonTruncation, and the cost/quality curve
import numpy as np

def truncate(embeddings, dims):
    """Take the first `dims` components and RE-NORMALISE.
    Skipping the renormalisation is a real bug -- the truncated
    vector no longer has unit length, so dot product stops being
    cosine similarity and long documents start winning."""
    out = embeddings[..., :dims]
    return out / np.linalg.norm(out, axis=-1, keepdims=True)

# Storage for 10 million chunks, float32
for d in (1536, 768, 512, 256, 128):
    gb = 10_000_000 * d * 4 / 1e9
    print(f"{d:5d} dims -> {gb:6.1f} GB")

# 1536 dims ->   61.4 GB
#  768 dims ->   30.7 GB
#  512 dims ->   20.5 GB
#  256 dims ->   10.2 GB   often ~97-99% of full-dim recall
#  128 dims ->    5.1 GB

# Combine with int8 quantisation for another 4x:
def quantize_int8(v, lo=-1.0, hi=1.0):
    return np.clip(np.round((v - lo) / (hi - lo) * 255 - 128), -128, 127).astype(np.int8)

# 256 dims in int8 = 2.6 GB for 10M chunks -- a 24x reduction from
# the 1536-dim float32 baseline, typically for a few points of recall.
The two-stage pattern this enables

Store both a truncated and a full vector. Search the cheap 256-dimension index to get 200 candidates, then re-score those candidates against the full 1536-dimension vectors. You get near-full-dimension accuracy with a fraction of the index memory and a much faster first pass. This is the same shape as binary-then-exact rescoring, and the same shape as retrieve-then-rerank — a coarse cheap filter followed by an expensive accurate one. Once you notice the pattern, you will see it everywhere in retrieval.

PythonEvaluate candidates on your own data
import numpy as np
from sentence_transformers import SentenceTransformer

# Your golden set: question -> the chunk ids that answer it.
# 50-200 real questions. This is the artefact that makes every
# later decision in Phase 4 measurable rather than aesthetic.
golden = [
    {"q": "how do I reset my password", "relevant": [12, 47]},
    {"q": "what is the refund window",  "relevant": [88]},
]

def recall_at_k(retrieved, relevant, k):
    hits = set(retrieved[:k]) & set(relevant)
    return len(hits) / len(relevant)

def mrr(retrieved, relevant):
    for rank, doc_id in enumerate(retrieved, start=1):
        if doc_id in relevant:
            return 1.0 / rank
    return 0.0

def evaluate(model_name, chunks, golden, query_prefix="", doc_prefix=""):
    model = SentenceTransformer(model_name)

    # Prefixes matter for E5/BGE-family models. Omitting them costs
    # several points of recall with no error message.
    doc_vecs = model.encode([doc_prefix + c for c in chunks],
                            normalize_embeddings=True, batch_size=64)

    r5 = r20 = rr = 0.0
    for case in golden:
        q = model.encode(query_prefix + case["q"], normalize_embeddings=True)
        ranked = np.argsort(-(doc_vecs @ q)).tolist()
        r5  += recall_at_k(ranked, case["relevant"], 5)
        r20 += recall_at_k(ranked, case["relevant"], 20)
        rr  += mrr(ranked, case["relevant"])

    n = len(golden)
    return {"model": model_name, "recall@5": r5/n, "recall@20": r20/n, "mrr": rr/n}

candidates = [
    ("BAAI/bge-small-en-v1.5",                    "Represent this sentence for searching relevant passages: ", ""),
    ("intfloat/e5-base-v2",                        "query: ", "passage: "),
    ("sentence-transformers/all-MiniLM-L6-v2",     "", ""),
    ("nomic-ai/nomic-embed-text-v1.5",             "search_query: ", "search_document: "),
]

for name, qp, dp in candidates:
    print(evaluate(name, chunks, golden, qp, dp))

# Also record: index size, p95 embedding latency, cost per million
# tokens. The best model on recall is not always the right choice.

Section 06 ProfessionalOperating embeddings

Vector spaces are not interchangeable — plan for migration

Two embedding models produce unrelated coordinate systems. Cosine similarity between a vector from model A and one from model B returns a number, and the number is meaningless. This is not a subtle degradation; it is noise that looks like a result.

Consequences you must design for: changing model means re-embedding the entire corpus; a provider silently updating an API model corrupts your index incrementally as new documents arrive; and mixing two models in one store produces a system that works for old queries and fails unpredictably for new ones.

Store the model identifier and version with every vector. Assert on it at query time. To migrate, write to a new index, backfill, verify recall on the golden set, then switch atomically. Never migrate in place.

Operational practices
ConcernPractice
BatchingEmbed 32–256 texts per call. Per-text calls are 10–50× slower and often rate-limited
CachingHash the text and cache the vector. Re-ingestion of unchanged documents becomes free
TruncationCount tokens and reject or split before embedding. Silent truncation is invisible data loss
Empty inputGuard against whitespace-only chunks — they produce degenerate vectors and NaN similarities
NormalisationNormalise once on write; use inner product thereafter
Self-hostingA small model on ONNX Runtime or an inference server is often cheaper and lower-latency than an API, and keeps data in your VPC
MonitoringTrack embedding latency, failure rate, and the distribution of top-1 similarity scores — a shift in that distribution is an early warning of a model or data change

Section 07 ProfessionalExercises

Do these — roughly one weekend
  1. Build a golden set of 50 questions with known relevant chunks from your own corpus. This artefact is used by every remaining page in Phase 4.
  2. Run the comparison script over four candidate models. Record recall@5, recall@20, MRR, index size and embedding latency in one table.
  3. Run an E5-family model with and without the query: / passage: prefixes. Measure the difference. Note that nothing warned you.
  4. Embed the same text with and without the attention mask applied during mean pooling. Compare the vectors and the resulting recall.
  5. Truncate a Matryoshka embedding to 768, 512, 256 and 128 dimensions. Plot recall@5 against index size and find your own knee point.
  6. Add int8 quantisation on top of truncation. Measure the additional recall loss against the additional saving.
  7. Retrieve with a bi-encoder, then re-rank the top 50 with a cross-encoder. Measure recall@5 before and after. This number is why §4.6 exists.
  8. Fine-tune a small embedding model on 500 domain pairs (Topic 2.5) and re-run your evaluation.
