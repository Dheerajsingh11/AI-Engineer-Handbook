Section 01 BeginnerStart from zero

Assumed knowledge

Vectors and embeddings (Topic 1.1) and tokenisation from the LLM side (Topic 1.3). This page is the classical NLP foundation those built on.

Natural Language Processing is teaching computers to work with human language. Long before LLMs, NLP was a mature field with a standard toolkit for turning messy text into something a machine could analyse. Modern LLMs absorbed much of this — but the vocabulary and the pipeline still explain a great deal about how language is handled, and you will meet these terms constantly.

The classic NLP pipeline

Traditional NLP processed text through a sequence of stages, each adding structure: split into tokens → normalise them → tag their grammatical roles → parse the sentence structure → identify the entities. Each stage was often a separate model. The big shift with LLMs is that a single model now does much of this implicitly — it learned grammar and entities from data without being told the stages exist. But the concepts remain the right way to think about language tasks, and the classic tools are still the pragmatic choice for many jobs.

The words, in plain English
Token
A unit of text — a word or subword.
Stemming / lemmatisation
Reducing words to a root: "running" → "run".
Part of speech (POS)
A word's grammatical role: noun, verb, adjective.
Parsing
Working out the grammatical structure of a sentence.
Named-entity recognition (NER)
Finding and labelling names of people, places, organisations, dates.
Word embedding
A vector representing a word's meaning (Topic 1.1).
Corpus
A large body of text used to train or analyse.

Section 02 IntermediateThe pipeline in practice

PythonClassic NLP with spaCy
import spacy
nlp = spacy.load("en_core_web_sm")

doc = nlp("Dr. Okonkwo joined Acme Corp in London last March.")

# Tokenisation + POS + lemma, all at once:
for token in doc:
    print(token.text, token.pos_, token.lemma_)
# Dr.      PROPN  Dr.
# joined   VERB   join      <- lemmatised
# London   PROPN  London

# Named-entity recognition -- who/what/where/when:
for ent in doc.ents:
    print(ent.text, ent.label_)
# Okonkwo   PERSON
# Acme Corp ORG
# London    GPE     (geo-political entity)
# last March DATE

# Dependency parse -- the grammatical structure (a tree):
# displacy.render(doc)  shows subject -> verb -> object relations

These tools are still useful. For extracting entities from millions of documents, tagging parts of speech, or building a fast, cheap, deterministic text pipeline, classic tools like spaCy are often the right choice — no API cost, no latency, fully reproducible. NER in particular is a workhorse: pulling names, dates and organisations out of documents is exactly what your ingestion pipeline (Topic 4.5) needs for metadata, and a small NER model does it faster and cheaper than an LLM. Reaching for an LLM for every text task is a common and expensive mistake; the classic toolkit still earns its place.

Section 03 IntermediateWord embeddings: the bridge to modern NLP

The single most important idea from classical NLP — the one that leads directly to LLMs — is the word embedding. You met the principle in Topic 1.1; here is its NLP lineage.

word2vec and the distributional hypothesis

In 2013, word2vec showed you could learn a vector for every word by a simple task: predict a word from its neighbours (or vice versa) across a huge corpus. The result placed words with similar meanings near each other in vector space — because, per the distributional hypothesis (Topic 1.1), words used in similar contexts mean similar things. Famously, the vectors even captured analogies: king − man + woman ≈ queen. GloVe reached similar embeddings from global co-occurrence statistics. This was the proof that meaning could be turned into geometry — the foundation everything in Phase 4 rests on, and the direct ancestor of the embeddings you use for RAG.

The evolution of word representations
ApproachIdeaLimitation it had
One-hot / bag-of-wordsCount words; no meaning"good" and "great" are unrelated
word2vec / GloVeOne vector per word, from contextOne vector per word — "bank" (river/money) collapsed
Contextual (ELMo, BERT)A word's vector depends on its sentenceHeavier to compute
LLM embeddings (Topic 4.2)Rich contextual representations of whole texts— the current state of the art

The key limitation word2vec had — one fixed vector per word — is exactly what contextual models (BERT, and then LLMs) fixed: they give "bank" a different vector in "river bank" than in "savings bank," because the vector depends on the whole sentence. That move from static to contextual embeddings is one of the main things that made modern NLP work, and it is why today's embedding models (Topic 4.2) represent meaning so much better.

The practical judgement

The LLM did not make classical NLP useless — it made it a choice. For a task an LLM does well and you run rarely, prompt the LLM. For a task you run millions of times where cost, latency and determinism matter — extracting entities from a document stream, classifying tickets at scale — a small classic model (spaCy NER, a fine-tuned classifier) is frequently the better engineering answer. Knowing the classical toolkit gives you a cheaper, faster option than reaching for an LLM every time, which is exactly the model-routing discipline from Topic 9.1 applied to text tasks. The engineer who knows both picks correctly; the one who only knows LLMs overpays.

Section 05 ProfessionalExercises

Do these — roughly an evening
  1. Run the spaCy pipeline on a paragraph. Inspect tokens, POS tags, the dependency parse and the entities.
  2. Extract all people, organisations and dates from a batch of documents with spaCy NER. Time it, then do the same with an LLM and compare cost and speed.
  3. Load pretrained word2vec or GloVe vectors. Find the nearest neighbours of a few words, and try the king − man + woman analogy.
  4. Take a polysemous word ("bank", "bat") and show that word2vec gives it one vector, then that a contextual model gives it different vectors in different sentences.
  5. Build a fast NER-based metadata extractor for an ingestion pipeline (Topic 4.5) — no LLM.
  6. For three text tasks you have, decide classic tool vs LLM using the Section 04 judgement, and justify each.
