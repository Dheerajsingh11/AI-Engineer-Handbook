12.4

GANs

Two networks locked in a forgery contest. This page explains the generative adversarial idea — a generator trying to fake data and a discriminator trying to catch it, improving each other — why the game is both powerful and famously unstable, what GANs are good at, and why diffusion models have largely overtaken them while GANs still win on speed.

Section 01 BeginnerStart from zero

Assumed knowledge

Neural network training (Topic 2.4) and the idea of generating data (Topic 12.3, VAEs). GANs are a strikingly different route to the same goal.

How do you train a network to generate realistic images when "realistic" is hard to define with a loss function? The Generative Adversarial Network answer, from 2014, was brilliant: don't define realism — have a second network learn to judge it. Set two networks against each other and let their competition drive both to excellence.

The forger and the detective

Picture a counterfeiter (the generator) trying to produce fake banknotes, and a detective (the discriminator) trying to tell fakes from real ones. They train together: every time the detective catches a fake, the forger learns to do better; every time a fake slips through, the detective learns to be sharper. Neither is given a rulebook for "realistic" — they bootstrap it from their contest. After enough rounds, the forger produces fakes the detective cannot distinguish from real — which means they look real to us too. That adversarial game is the entire idea.

The words, in plain English
Generator
The network that creates fake data from random noise.
Discriminator
The network that judges whether data is real or generated.
Adversarial
The two networks have opposite goals; one's gain is the other's loss.
Latent vector
The random noise the generator turns into an output; different noise, different output.
Mode collapse
A failure where the generator produces only a few outputs, ignoring variety.
Convergence
The (delicate) point where the game reaches a useful balance.

Section 02 IntermediateThe training game

PythonThe GAN training loop
for real_batch in data:
    # --- Train the DISCRIMINATOR: get better at spotting fakes -----
    noise = torch.randn(batch_size, latent_dim)
    fake = generator(noise).detach()             # don't train G on this step
    d_loss = bce(discriminator(real_batch), ones) \
           + bce(discriminator(fake),      zeros)  # real=1, fake=0
    d_loss.backward(); d_optim.step()

    # --- Train the GENERATOR: get better at fooling D -------------
    noise = torch.randn(batch_size, latent_dim)
    fake = generator(noise)
    # G wins when D thinks the fake is REAL -- so its target is ones.
    g_loss = bce(discriminator(fake), ones)
    g_loss.backward(); g_optim.step()

# The two losses pull against each other. Neither should "win" --
# you want a tense equilibrium, which is exactly what makes GANs
# hard to train (Section 03).

Notice there is no target image and no "realism" loss — the discriminator is the loss function, and it improves over time. Early on the generator produces noise and the discriminator easily catches it; as they co-evolve, the fakes get sharper and the judge gets pickier, until the generator is producing convincing samples. This co-evolution is what makes GANs elegant, and it is also the source of all their difficulty.

Section 03 AdvancedWhy GANs are famously hard to train

The adversarial game is a delicate balance, and balances are fragile. GANs earned a reputation for being temperamental, and the reasons are structural.

The classic GAN failure modes
ProblemWhat happensWhy
Mode collapseGenerator produces only a few outputsIt found something that fools D and stopped exploring
Non-convergenceTraining oscillates, never settlesTwo objectives chasing each other in circles
ImbalanceOne network overpowers the otherIf D gets too good, G gets no useful signal
Vanishing gradientsG stops learningA perfect D gives G nothing to improve against
Why balance is the whole battle

A normal network minimises a fixed loss — a stable target. A GAN chases a moving target: the generator improves against a discriminator that is itself improving. If either races ahead, training breaks — too strong a discriminator starves the generator of signal; too strong a generator makes the discriminator useless. Years of research went into stabilising this: better loss functions (Wasserstein GAN), architectural rules (DCGAN), and tricks like gradient penalties. The lesson generalises beyond GANs: any system built on competing objectives is inherently harder to train than one minimising a single fixed goal — which is a large part of why the field ultimately preferred diffusion (Topic 12.5) for image generation.

Section 04 ProfessionalWhat GANs are good at — and where diffusion won

GANs vs diffusion — the honest state of play

For general high-quality image generation, diffusion models (Topic 12.5) have largely overtaken GANs: they are more stable to train, cover the full variety of the data (no mode collapse), and produce more diverse, controllable output. That is why Stable Diffusion, DALL-E and Midjourney are diffusion-based, not GAN-based. But GANs are not obsolete — their decisive advantage is speed: a GAN generates in a single forward pass, while diffusion needs many steps (Topic 12.5). For real-time or high-throughput generation, GANs and GAN-diffusion hybrids remain competitive. The takeaway: diffusion for quality and control, GANs when you need generation to be fast.

A word on deepfakes. The same technology that generates faces for research generates convincing fake images and video of real people. This is a live ethical and security concern (Topic 6.4, Topic 5.6) — detection, provenance and consent are active problems. If you build with generative visual models, the misuse potential is part of your responsibility, not an afterthought.

Section 05 ProfessionalExercises

Do these — roughly a weekend
  1. Train a small GAN on MNIST. Watch the generated digits improve from noise over epochs.
  2. Deliberately make the discriminator train much faster than the generator. Observe training break — that is the imbalance problem.
  3. Reproduce mode collapse: watch the generator start producing only one or two digits. Then try a fix (label smoothing, a lower D learning rate).
  4. Generate from the same generator with different random latent vectors. Interpolate between two — see the output morph.
  5. Compare a GAN and a small diffusion model (Topic 12.5) on generation quality and speed. Reproduce the Section 04 trade-off.
  6. Read about deepfake detection and write a paragraph on the provenance problem (Topic 6.4).
