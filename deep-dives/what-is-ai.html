What Is AI: ML, DL & GenAI

The map of the whole field, for someone starting from nothing. This page defines AI, machine learning, deep learning and generative AI and shows how they nest; separates the types of AI (narrow, general, super) from the science-fiction versions; and gives you the vocabulary to place every other topic in this handbook.

Section 01 BeginnerStart from zero

Assumed knowledge

None whatsoever. This is the front door to the entire handbook. If you are new to AI, start here; every other topic assumes the vocabulary this page establishes.

"AI" is used to mean a dozen different things, which makes the field sound more confusing than it is. The single most useful thing you can learn first is how the main terms fit together — because they are not competing ideas, they are nested ones, each a special case of the one above.

The four terms, from broadest to narrowest
TermMeansExample
Artificial IntelligenceAny technique that makes a machine do something we would call "intelligent"A chess program; a route planner; a spam filter
  ↳ Machine LearningAI that learns from data instead of being explicitly programmedA spam filter trained on labelled emails
    ↳ Deep LearningML using neural networks with many layersImage recognition; speech-to-text
      ↳ Generative AIDeep learning that creates new contentChatGPT; image generators
The nesting is the whole point

Generative AI is a kind of deep learning, which is a kind of machine learning, which is a kind of AI. So an LLM is all four at once. When someone says "AI" today they usually mean the innermost box — generative AI — but the outer boxes are older, larger, and still everywhere: your bank's fraud detection, your email's spam filter, and your maps app are all AI, most of it not generative. This handbook lives mostly in the two innermost boxes, but it is worth knowing the whole nesting so you can place any tool you meet.

Section 02 BeginnerThe shift that made it work

The deepest idea in the whole field is a reversal of how software normally works.

Two ways to make a computer do something
Traditional programmingMachine learning
You writeThe rulesExamples (data + answers)
The computer producesAnswers, by following your rulesThe rules, by learning from your examples
To improve itWrite more rulesShow it more examples
Good forProblems with clear rules (tax, payroll)Problems too fuzzy to write rules for (recognising a cat)

Why this matters. Some problems have rules you can write down — calculate a tax, sort a list. Others do not: nobody can write the rules that distinguish a photo of a cat from a photo of a dog, yet a three-year-old does it instantly. Machine learning was the breakthrough for exactly these fuzzy problems: instead of writing impossible rules, you show the computer thousands of labelled examples and let it work out the pattern. Generative AI is the same trick pointed at creation — show a model enough text and it learns to produce text.

The words, in plain English
Model
The thing a machine learns — the learned pattern, plus the rule for using it.
Training
The process of learning that pattern from data.
Inference
Using a trained model to get an answer. What you do every day.
Data
The examples a model learns from. More and cleaner is usually better.
Neural network
The kind of model deep learning uses — layers of simple units, loosely inspired by the brain.
Algorithm
A step-by-step procedure. "The learning algorithm" is how the model is trained.

Section 03 IntermediateTypes of AI — the real ones and the sci-fi ones

You will hear "narrow AI", "AGI" and "superintelligence". These describe how general an AI is, and it is important to know which exist today and which are aspirations.

AI by breadth of ability
TypeMeansExists?
Narrow AI (ANI)Good at one specific thingYes — everything today is this
General AI (AGI)Human-level across any taskNot yet; actively pursued and debated
Superintelligence (ASI)Beyond human across the boardHypothetical
Everything you will build is narrow AI

Even the most capable chatbot is narrow AI: astonishingly broad-seeming, but still a system trained to predict text, without goals, understanding or awareness of the kind the words "general" and "super" imply. Marketing blurs this constantly. When you read that a model "understands" or "thinks", read it as a useful shorthand for a statistical process (Topic 1.2), not a literal claim. Keeping that distinction sharp will make you a clearer thinker about what these systems can and cannot do — and a more honest engineer.

A second, more useful classification is by how the system learns — the paradigms you will actually work with:

AI by how it learns (covered in Topic 2.3)
ParadigmLearns fromExample
SupervisedLabelled examplesSpam detection
UnsupervisedUnlabelled data — finds structureCustomer segments
ReinforcementTrial and error with rewardsGame-playing; robot control
Self-supervisedLabels derived from the data itselfHow LLMs are trained (Topic 1.3)

Section 04 IntermediateWhy generative AI changed everything

AI existed for decades before 2022 — so why the sudden explosion? Three things converged, and understanding them explains the moment we are in.

  1. The transformer (2017). A neural-network design (Topic 1.3) that scales beautifully with data and compute, and handles language far better than anything before it.
  2. Scale. Training these models on enormous text corpora with enormous compute produced capabilities nobody explicitly programmed — and, surprisingly, kept improving as the scale grew.
  3. The interface. Chat made that capability usable by anyone, with no code. ChatGPT put a research capability in front of hundreds of millions of people overnight.
What generative AI is, mechanically

Under the excitement, a generative language model does something simple to state: it predicts the next word, over and over (Topic 1.2). Trained on enough text, that single ability — guess what comes next — produces essays, code, translations and conversations. It is not looking things up in a database and not reasoning like a person; it is sampling likely continuations. Holding that plain picture in mind, while still respecting how capable the result is, is the mark of someone who actually understands the technology rather than the hype around it.

Handbook areaPart of the field

You do not need all of it for most jobs. An applied AI engineer building with LLMs lives mostly in the generative-AI and production boxes — prompting, RAG, agents, serving. The broader deep-learning and classical-ML topics are worth understanding so you can recognise the right tool and read the field, but you will use a fraction of them daily. This map is for orientation, not a checklist you must complete before starting.

Section 06 ProfessionalExercises

Do these — roughly an hour
  1. For five AI products you use (maps, spam filter, a recommender, a chatbot, autocomplete), classify each as ML / deep learning / generative, and as narrow/general.
  2. Explain to a non-technical friend, in two sentences, the difference between traditional programming and machine learning.
  3. Find one news headline that uses "AI" to mean generative AI and one that uses it to mean older ML. Notice how often the word is doing different jobs.
  4. Write one plain-English sentence explaining what a generative language model actually does mechanically. Avoid the words "understand" and "think".
  5. Pick any later topic in this handbook and place it on the Section 05 map. Confirm you know which box it lives in.
