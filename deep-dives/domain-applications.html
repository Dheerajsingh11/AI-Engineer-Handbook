17.3

AI Across Domains

AI where the stakes and rules differ — healthcare, finance, legal, and beyond. This page surveys how AI is applied across regulated and high-consequence domains, what changes when a wrong answer has real costs, the recurring pattern of augmentation over automation, and the domain-specific constraints an engineer must respect.

Section 01 BeginnerStart from zero

Assumed knowledge

The whole handbook, loosely — especially RAG (Phase 4), safety (Topics 16.1–2) and governance (Topic 6.4). This page is about applying it all in specific fields.

AI is being applied across nearly every industry, but the engineering changes dramatically depending on the domain — specifically, on what happens when the AI is wrong. A wrong movie recommendation is a shrug; a wrong medical dosage, loan denial, or legal citation is a catastrophe. This page is about how the stakes and rules of a domain reshape how you build.

The pattern that holds across high-stakes domains

In consequential fields, the winning pattern is almost always augmentation, not automation: the AI assists a qualified human who remains responsible and makes the final call, rather than deciding autonomously. AI drafts, a doctor decides. AI flags, an analyst reviews. AI researches, a lawyer verifies. This is not timidity — it is the correct engineering response to the combination of high stakes, imperfect models (hallucination, Topic 1.3), and legal accountability (Topic 6.4). The valuable systems in these domains make the expert faster and better, not redundant. Building for augmentation — with the human in the loop and clearly in charge — is the through-line of applied AI where it matters most.

The hallucinated-citation cautionary tale

The legal domain produced the field's most instructive failure: lawyers who submitted AI-generated briefs citing court cases that did not exist — the model fabricated plausible-looking citations (Topic 1.3), the lawyers did not verify, and they were sanctioned. The lesson generalises to every high-stakes domain: a fluent, confident, wrong answer is the characteristic AI failure, and in a consequential field it must be caught by design. The fixes are exactly the handbook's tools — RAG so answers come from real sources, mandatory citation validation (check that cited sources exist, Topic 4.7), and a human expert who verifies before acting. Never deploy AI into a high-stakes domain trusting the model not to hallucinate; assume it will, and build the verification that catches it.

Domain expertise is not optional

You cannot build good AI for a field you do not understand, and you cannot evaluate it either — a plausible-sounding medical or legal answer needs a qualified person to judge whether it is actually correct. So building for a domain is inherently collaborative: pair with domain experts to define what "correct" means, to build the evaluation set (Topic 4.7), to review outputs, and to catch the failures a generalist would miss. The engineer brings the AI systems; the domain expert brings the judgement about what is right and what is dangerous. Systems built without that partnership are the ones that produce confident, well-formatted, wrong answers that no one qualified caught.

Section 04 ProfessionalWhere the opportunity is

Vertical depth is where durable value lives

Recall from Topic 11.3 that a thin wrapper around a model API has no moat — the model is a commodity. The durable value is in vertical depth: deep integration with a specific domain's data, workflows, regulations and experts. An AI system that genuinely understands the nuances of insurance claims, or clinical documentation, or contract law — because it was built with domain data, domain evaluation, and domain experts — is defensible in a way a general chatbot is not. For an engineer building a career or a product, combining AI skills with deep knowledge of one domain is a strong position: the AI skills are increasingly common, but AI-skills-plus-real-domain-understanding is rare and valuable. Your existing industry experience, whatever it is, is an asset here, not a distraction from the "pure AI" path.

Section 05 ProfessionalExercises

Do these — roughly a weekend
  1. Pick a domain you know. List what would change about a RAG system if a wrong answer had serious consequences (Section 02).
  2. Build citation validation for a RAG system: verify that every cited source actually exists and supports the claim (Topic 4.7).
  3. Design an augmentation workflow (AI drafts, human decides) for a high-stakes task. Specify where the human reviews and can override.
  4. Identify the privacy/regulatory regime for a domain (HIPAA, GDPR, financial rules) and list what it requires of your system (Topic 6.4).
  5. Build a domain-specific golden eval set and have a domain expert (or a proxy) review it.
  6. Write a short pitch for an AI product that combines your existing domain knowledge with your new AI skills (Section 04).
