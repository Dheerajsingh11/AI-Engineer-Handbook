14.8

Causal Inference

Telling correlation from causation. This page covers causal inference — the discipline of answering “what happens if we intervene?” rather than merely “what predicts what?” — why standard ML cannot answer it, the gold standard (experiments), the observational methods when you cannot experiment, and where it matters to you.

Section 01 BeginnerStart from zero

Assumed knowledge

Probability & statistics (Topic 1.2) and classical ML (Topic 2.3). This page is about a question ML normally does not answer: cause, not just prediction.

Machine learning is brilliant at prediction — "given these features, what is the likely outcome?" But businesses constantly ask a different, harder question: "if we change this, what will happen?" Will this feature increase retention? Does this drug work? Did the discount cause the sale, or would the customer have bought anyway? That is causal inference, and a model that only found correlations cannot answer it.

The distinction that trips everyone up

Correlation is not causation — you know the slogan, but the failure is subtle and expensive. Ice-cream sales correlate with drownings (both caused by hot weather, the confounder). A model predicting drownings from ice-cream sales would be accurate and useless for policy: banning ice cream saves no one. Prediction asks "what tends to occur together?"; causation asks "what happens if I intervene?" Most costly analytics mistakes come from answering the first while believing you answered the second.

The words, in plain English
Confounder
A hidden cause of both the treatment and the outcome (weather, above).
Treatment
The intervention whose effect you want (a feature, a drug, a discount).
Counterfactual
What would have happened without the treatment — never directly observed.
ATE
Average Treatment Effect — the average causal impact of the treatment.
RCT
Randomised Controlled Trial — the gold-standard experiment.

Section 02 IntermediateThe fundamental problem

Causal inference has one core difficulty: you can never observe both outcomes for the same unit. A customer either got the discount or did not; you never see the same customer in both worlds.

The Average Treatment Effect — and why it's hard ATE = E[ Y(1) − Y(0) ] Y(1) is the outcome with treatment, Y(0) without. For any individual you see only one of them — the other is the unobserved counterfactual. This is the "fundamental problem of causal inference." We can only estimate the ATE across a group, and only if the treated and untreated groups are otherwise comparable (Section 03).
Simpson's paradox

A trend can reverse when you account for a confounder. A treatment can look better overall yet be worse within every subgroup (or vice versa), purely because the groups differ in some hidden variable. Simpson's paradox is the sharpest reminder that an aggregate correlation can point the opposite way to the truth — and why "we A/B tested it" beats "we looked at the historical data" almost every time.

Section 03 IntermediateThe gold standard: experiments

The cleanest way to get causation is to randomise. If you assign the treatment by a coin flip, the treated and untreated groups are — on average — identical in every other respect, so any outcome difference must be caused by the treatment.

Why randomisation is magic

Randomisation breaks the link between the treatment and every confounder, known and unknown. That is what no amount of clever modelling on observational data can guarantee — you can only adjust for confounders you thought of and measured. This is exactly why the A/B test (Topic 19.1) is the backbone of product decisions: assign users randomly to variant A or B, measure the outcome, and the difference is causal. For an AI engineer, "ship it behind an A/B test" is not bureaucracy — it is the one method that actually answers "did my change help?"

Section 04 AdvancedWhen you can't experiment

Often you cannot run an experiment — it is unethical, too slow, or the data is already collected. Then you estimate causation from observational data by emulating a randomised comparison, adjusting for the confounders you can measure.

Observational causal methods
MethodIdea
Propensity-score matchingPair each treated unit with an untreated one that was equally likely to be treated
Regression / covariate adjustmentControl for confounders as model features (only works if you measured them)
Difference-in-differencesCompare the change in treated vs untreated groups over time
Instrumental variablesUse a variable that affects treatment but not the outcome directly
Causal graphs (DAGs)Draw the assumed cause arrows to decide what to adjust for (and what not to)
Uplift modelling — where ML meets causation

The place these two worlds meet is uplift (treatment-effect) modelling: instead of predicting "who will buy," predict "whose behaviour will change because of the intervention." A customer who would buy anyway is wasted spend; a customer who buys only if targeted is the one to target. This reframes a marketing/retention problem from prediction to causation, and it is a genuinely valuable, under-used skill. The catch that never goes away: every observational method rests on the assumption that you measured all the important confounders — and you can never fully verify it. State the assumption, and prefer an experiment when you can run one.

Section 05 ProfessionalWhy an AI engineer should care

You will meet causation constantly, usually disguised. Evaluating a change (does the new model actually improve retention, or just correlate with a good week?) is a causal question — answer it with an A/B test (Topic 19.1), not an offline metric. Recommenders and pricing (Topic 14.6) are causal at heart: recommending what someone would have found anyway adds no value. Feature-importance from a model (Topic 16.3) tells you what predicts the outcome, not what would change it — confusing the two leads to acting on a confounder. And bias analysis (Topic 16.1) is often a causal question about why a disparity exists. You do not need to be a causal-inference specialist, but knowing when a question is causal — and that prediction alone cannot answer it — is what stops you from confidently shipping the wrong conclusion.

Section 06 ProfessionalExercises

Do these — think in counterfactuals
  1. Construct a Simpson's-paradox dataset (a treatment that helps overall but hurts in every subgroup). Feel how the confounder flips it.
  2. Simulate an A/B test: randomly assign, compare outcomes, and recover the true treatment effect you built in.
  3. Take the same data but assign treatment non-randomly (confounded). Show the naive comparison is now biased.
  4. Fix that bias with propensity-score matching; compare your estimate to the true effect.
  5. Draw a causal DAG for a real decision (e.g. "does our onboarding email increase retention?"). Identify the confounders you'd need to control.
  6. Build a simple uplift model: predict who changes behaviour under treatment, not who converts. Contrast the targeting it suggests with a plain propensity model.
