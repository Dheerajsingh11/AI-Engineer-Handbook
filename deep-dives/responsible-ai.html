Responsible AI & Ethics

Building AI that does not harm the people it touches. This page covers the practical pillars of responsible AI — fairness, transparency, accountability, privacy — where bias enters a system and how to measure it, and the concrete engineering practices that turn good intentions into a system you can defend.

Section 01 BeginnerStart from zero

Assumed knowledge

Evaluation metrics (Topic 1.2) and governance basics (Topic 6.4). This page is the ethics-and-fairness companion to the legal governance page.

AI systems make and shape decisions that affect people — who gets a loan, a job interview, a diagnosis, a piece of content. Responsible AI is the practice of building those systems so they are fair, transparent, accountable and safe for the people they touch. It is not a compliance afterthought; it is engineering that prevents real harm, and increasingly a legal requirement (Topic 6.4).

The pillars of responsible AI
PillarQuestion it asks
FairnessDoes it treat different groups equitably?
TransparencyCan we explain what it does and why?
AccountabilityWho is responsible when it goes wrong?
PrivacyDoes it respect people's data? (Topic 6.4)
Safety & robustnessDoes it fail gracefully and resist misuse?
Human oversightCan a person understand, contest and override it?
The core reframing

An AI model does exactly what its data and objective push it toward — not what you intended, and not what is fair. If the training data reflects historical bias, the model learns and often amplifies that bias, at scale and with a veneer of objectivity that makes it harder to challenge. Responsible AI is the discipline of noticing this gap between "what the model optimises" and "what is right," measuring it, and closing it deliberately. It requires the same rigour as the rest of engineering — you cannot fix what you do not measure.

The words, in plain English
Bias
Systematic unfairness toward certain groups.
Fairness
Equitable treatment across groups — with several competing definitions.
Protected attribute
A characteristic the law protects: race, gender, age, disability.
Disparate impact
A neutral-seeming system that harms a group more.
Proxy
A feature that stands in for a protected attribute (postcode for race).
Human-in-the-loop
A person reviewing or able to override the AI.

Section 02 IntermediateWhere bias enters

Bias is not one thing that sneaks in at one place — it enters at every stage, and knowing where lets you look for it.

The sources of bias
StageHow bias entersExample
Data collectionUnrepresentative samplesA face dataset mostly of one demographic
Historical dataPast discrimination baked inHiring data reflecting who was hired before
LabellingHuman labellers' biasesSubjective "toxicity" labels varying by annotator
FeaturesProxies for protected attributesPostcode standing in for race
ObjectiveOptimising the wrong thingMaximising engagement → amplifying outrage
DeploymentUsed on a population unlike the training oneA model trained in one country used in another
Removing the protected attribute does not remove the bias

The intuitive fix — "just don't use race/gender as a feature" — does not work, because other features act as proxies. Postcode correlates with race; first name with gender; shopping patterns with age. A model denied the protected attribute directly will reconstruct it from proxies and discriminate anyway — a phenomenon that has caused real harm in deployed systems. Fairness therefore cannot be achieved by omission; it must be measured on outcomes across groups and actively corrected. This is why "we don't collect race" is not a fairness strategy — and why, paradoxically, you often need the protected attribute to test for bias even if you never use it to predict.

Section 03 AdvancedMeasuring fairness

Fairness has several mathematical definitions — and, importantly, they can conflict.

Common fairness definitions
DefinitionRequires
Demographic parityEqual positive rates across groups
Equal opportunityEqual true-positive rates across groups
Equalised oddsEqual true- and false-positive rates
CalibrationA given score means the same thing for every group
You cannot satisfy all fairness definitions at once

A striking, proven result: several reasonable fairness definitions are mathematically incompatible — you cannot have calibration and equalised odds simultaneously (except in trivial cases). This means fairness is not a box to tick; it is a choice about which notion matters for your context, made with domain experts and stakeholders, not by an engineer alone. A lending model and a medical screening tool may rightly choose different definitions. The engineering task is to measure outcomes across groups, surface the trade-offs explicitly, and make the choice transparent and defensible — not to pretend there is one neutral "fair."

Your part in it, as an engineer

Responsible AI is a team and organisational responsibility, but you as the engineer are often the first — sometimes only — person positioned to notice a problem: an unbalanced dataset, a proxy feature, a metric that optimises the wrong thing, a group the system fails. The practical mandate: build the measurement in from the start (fairness audits alongside your normal evals, Topic 4.7), document honestly what the system does and does not do, raise concerns rather than assuming someone else will, and keep a human in the loop for decisions with real consequences. These are engineering practices, not moral lectures — and doing them is what makes an AI system defensible when, not if, it is questioned.

Section 05 ProfessionalExercises

Do these — roughly a weekend
  1. Audit a model on a dataset with a protected attribute (e.g. the Adult income dataset). Measure positive rate and TPR across groups.
  2. Show that removing the protected attribute does not remove the disparity — the model reconstructs it from proxies (Section 02).
  3. Use Fairlearn to try two mitigations (reweighting, threshold adjustment). Note the accuracy-fairness trade-off.
  4. Pick two fairness definitions and show a model can satisfy one but not the other (Section 03).
  5. Write a model card for a system you have built: purpose, data, limitations, fairness testing, intended and out-of-scope uses.
  6. Red-team an LLM feature for harms — biased outputs, stereotyping — and document what you find (Topic 5.6).
