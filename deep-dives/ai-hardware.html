AI Hardware & Accelerators

The chips AI runs on, and how to choose and pay for them. This page covers the AI hardware landscape — GPUs, TPUs, NPUs and inference ASICs — why VRAM is the constraint that governs everything, the economics of renting versus buying compute, and how to size the hardware for a model without guessing.

Section 01 BeginnerStart from zero

Assumed knowledge

GPU computing (Topic 2.6) and serving/quantisation (Topic 6.1). This page is the landscape and economics layer above the CUDA mechanics.

Every AI system runs on physical chips that someone rents or buys, and those choices dominate the cost and feasibility of a project. You do not need to design silicon — but you do need to answer, credibly, "what hardware does this need, and what will it cost?" This page gives you that literacy.

The one number that governs everything: VRAM

Before FLOPS, before price, ask: does the model fit in memory? A model's weights, its activations, and (for training) its gradients and optimiser state must all fit in the accelerator's VRAM, or it simply will not run. This single constraint decides which chip you need, whether you must quantise (Topic 18.3), and whether one device suffices or you need many (Topic 9.6). A rough rule: a model needs about 2 GB of VRAM per billion parameters at 16-bit for inference (so a 7B model ≈ 14 GB), and several times that to train. Memory, not raw speed, is the wall you hit first — so hardware selection starts there.

The words, in plain English
GPU
The general-purpose AI workhorse (NVIDIA dominates via CUDA, Topic 2.6).
TPU
Google's custom AI chip, offered through its cloud.
NPU
A small AI accelerator built into phones and laptops (Topic 19.4).
ASIC
A chip built for one job — e.g. inference-only accelerators.
VRAM
The accelerator's memory. The hard limit on model size.
FLOPS
Floating-point operations per second — raw compute throughput.

Why NVIDIA is the default. The hardware matters, but the software ecosystem matters more: NVIDIA's CUDA (Topic 2.6), cuDNN and the entire stack of libraries and frameworks built on them is why almost everything "just works" on NVIDIA and is a porting effort elsewhere. This is the real "moat." Alternatives (TPUs, Trainium, AMD) can be cheaper or faster for specific workloads, but you pay in ecosystem friction — fewer libraries, more debugging, less community. For most teams the pragmatic default is NVIDIA GPUs, reaching for alternatives only when scale makes the cost saving worth the friction.

Section 03 IntermediateTraining vs inference hardware

The two workloads have different appetites, and conflating them wastes money.

Different jobs, different chips
TrainingInference
RunsOnce (or occasionally), for a long timeConstantly, per request, forever
Memory needHuge — weights + gradients + optimiser + activationsModest — weights + activations only
Precisionbf16 / fp16 mixed (Topic 2.6)int8 / int4 quantised (Topics 6.1, 18.3)
Scale patternMany chips, tightly connected (Topic 9.6)Many independent replicas
Cost lives inA big one-off billThe per-request bill that never stops
For most teams, inference is the real bill

Training a model is a dramatic, visible cost — but most companies do not train foundation models; they serve them. And inference cost, paid on every single request forever, quietly dwarfs a one-off fine-tune over a product's life. This reframes hardware strategy: the high-leverage work is usually making inference cheap — quantise aggressively (Topic 18.3), pick inference-optimised chips, batch and cache (Topic 19.3) — not chasing the biggest training rig. Match the chip to the workload: rent a monster briefly to train or fine-tune, then serve on the cheapest hardware that meets your latency bar.

Section 04 AdvancedRent vs buy, and sizing

Almost everyone rents compute from a cloud, and for good reasons — but knowing the trade-off, and how to size, is what keeps the bill sane.

TextSizing a model to hardware — the back-of-envelope
Inference VRAM  ≈  params × bytes_per_param  ×  ~1.2 (overhead)
  7B model, fp16  :  7e9 × 2  ≈  14 GB   -> fits one 24 GB GPU
  7B model, int4  :  7e9 × 0.5 ≈  3.5 GB -> fits a laptop GPU (Topic 18.3)
  70B model, fp16 :  70e9 × 2 ≈ 140 GB  -> needs 2x 80 GB, or quantise

Training VRAM   ≈  inference × ~4-6   (gradients + optimiser + activations)
  -> 7B full fine-tune needs ~80 GB+;  LoRA/QLoRA (Topic 3.5) fits far less

Also add: KV cache grows with context length × batch (Topics 1.3, 18.4)
The sizing instinct

These estimates are what let you answer the interview and planning question — "what will it take to run this?" — without guessing. Start from parameters and precision to get inference VRAM; multiply for training; add the KV cache for long contexts. The result tells you the chip, the count, and whether quantisation is mandatory. It also reveals the cheapest path: a smaller or quantised model that fits one commodity GPU is often far more economical than a marginally better model that needs two expensive ones. Hardware sizing is where model choice, compression and cost meet — and doing it in your head is a mark of an engineer who has shipped.

Section 05 ProfessionalMaking the call

In practice the decision flows the same way each time: estimate VRAM from the model and precision; decide train vs serve and match the chip class; rent by default, buy only for steady heavy load; and drive inference cost down with quantisation, batching and caching before reaching for bigger hardware. Keep an eye on the moving parts — new chips and cheaper inference options appear constantly — but anchor on the fundamentals, which do not change: memory is the constraint, inference is the recurring bill, the ecosystem is a real cost, and the smallest model that meets the bar on the cheapest hardware that fits is almost always the right answer. This is systems economics applied to AI, and it is squarely part of the engineer's job (Topics 10.1, 19.3).

Section 06 ProfessionalExercises

Do these — think in VRAM and dollars
  1. Size three models (a 7B, a 13B, a 70B) for inference at fp16 and int4. Say which hardware each needs.
  2. Estimate the VRAM to full-fine-tune a 7B model, then to QLoRA it. Explain the gap (Topics 3.5, 2.6).
  3. Price one week of a rented H100 vs the amortised cost of owning one. Find the utilisation where buying wins.
  4. For a product doing 1M requests/day, estimate the monthly inference bill, then halve it with quantisation and caching.
  5. Pick a real workload and choose the chip class end to end, justifying VRAM, train-vs-serve, and rent-vs-buy.
  6. Add a long context (say 128k) to a sizing estimate and account for the KV cache. See how it changes the hardware.
