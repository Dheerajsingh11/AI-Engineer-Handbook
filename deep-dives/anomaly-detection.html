Anomaly Detection

Finding the needle that does not belong. This page covers detecting the rare, the unusual, the fraudulent — why it is different from ordinary classification, the main approaches (statistical, distance, density, isolation, autoencoder), the extreme-imbalance evaluation trap, and where it meets your AI work in monitoring model behaviour and catching bad data.

Section 01 BeginnerStart from zero

Assumed knowledge

Classical ML and the precision/recall metrics (Topics 2.3, 1.2), and clustering (Topic 14.3).

Anomaly detection is finding the data points that do not fit the pattern — fraudulent transactions among millions of normal ones, a failing server among healthy ones, a defective part on a production line. It powers fraud detection, security monitoring, predictive maintenance and quality control.

Why it is not just classification

You might think "just train a classifier: normal vs anomaly." But anomalies have two properties that break ordinary classification. First, they are extremely rare — often well under 1% — so a classifier can score 99.9% accuracy by calling everything normal (the base-rate trap, Topic 1.2). Second, they are diverse and unknown — new kinds of fraud appear that look nothing like past fraud, so you often cannot learn "what anomalies look like" from examples. The winning reframing: instead of learning what anomalies look like, learn what normal looks like, and flag anything that deviates. That shift is the heart of the field.

The words, in plain English
Anomaly / outlier
A data point that differs markedly from the rest.
Novelty
A new pattern not seen in training — often what you are hunting.
Normal profile
A model of what typical data looks like.
Anomaly score
How unusual a point is; you threshold it to decide.
Contamination
The (estimated) fraction of data that is anomalous.
False positive
A normal point wrongly flagged — the operational cost of most systems.

Two elegant ideas worth knowing

Isolation Forest is delightfully simple: anomalies are, by definition, few and different, so they are easy to isolate — a few random splits separate an outlier, while normal points take many. The average number of splits to isolate a point becomes its anomaly score. Autoencoders (Topic 12.3) take the "learn normal" idea literally: train one to reconstruct normal data, and it will rebuild normal points well but fail on anomalies it never learned — high reconstruction error means anomaly. Both illustrate the reframing from Section 01: you never need labelled anomalies, only a model of normality.

PythonIsolation Forest and autoencoder detection
from sklearn.ensemble import IsolationForest

# Learn "normal" from mostly-normal data; score how anomalous each is.
detector = IsolationForest(contamination=0.01)   # ~1% expected anomalous
detector.fit(X_train)
scores = detector.decision_function(X_test)      # lower = more anomalous
flags  = detector.predict(X_test)                # -1 = anomaly, 1 = normal

# Autoencoder version (Topic 12.3): train on normal data only,
# then flag high reconstruction error.
recon = autoencoder(X_test)
error = ((X_test - recon) ** 2).mean(axis=1)
anomalies = error > threshold                    # poorly rebuilt = anomalous

Section 03 AdvancedThe evaluation trap, sharpened

Accuracy is meaningless here — and so are naive thresholds

With anomalies at 0.1%, a model that flags nothing is 99.9% accurate and completely useless (Topic 1.2). You must evaluate with precision, recall and especially PR-AUC (the precision-recall curve), which ignores the vast pile of true negatives and focuses on how well you catch the rare positives. Then comes the real engineering decision: the precision-recall trade-off is a business call. Fraud detection tolerates false positives (better to review a few good transactions than miss fraud) — favour recall. A system that auto-blocks accounts cannot — a false positive locks out a real customer — so favour precision. There is no universally right threshold; you set it by the relative cost of the two errors (Topic 1.2), and that cost is domain knowledge, not maths.

Section 04 ProfessionalWhere it meets your AI-engineering work

Anomaly detection guards your AI systems

Beyond classic fraud and monitoring, anomaly detection is a tool for keeping your own AI systems healthy. Data quality: flag anomalous documents entering your ingestion pipeline (Topic 4.5) — a corrupted file, an injection attempt, an off-distribution input. Embedding monitoring: detect drift in the distribution of embedding similarities, an early warning that your model or data changed (Topics 4.2, 5.5). Agent behaviour: flag anomalous agent trajectories — a runaway cost, an unusual tool-call pattern — as a safety signal (Topic 8.10). Model outputs: catch responses that deviate from normal (a spike in refusals or output length) as a sign of a model change (Topic 5.5). The "model normal, flag deviations" instinct is directly useful for operating AI in production.

Section 05 ProfessionalExercises

Do these — roughly a weekend
  1. Run Isolation Forest on a fraud dataset. Report PR-AUC, precision and recall — never accuracy.
  2. Train an autoencoder on normal data only, then flag high-reconstruction-error points. Compare to Isolation Forest.
  3. Sweep the anomaly threshold and plot precision against recall. Choose an operating point for (a) fraud review and (b) auto-blocking — and justify the difference.
  4. Use DBSCAN noise points (Topic 14.3) as an anomaly detector. Compare.
  5. Build a data-quality check for an ingestion pipeline that flags anomalous documents (Topic 4.5).
  6. Monitor the distribution of embedding similarities over time and detect an injected drift (Topic 5.5).
