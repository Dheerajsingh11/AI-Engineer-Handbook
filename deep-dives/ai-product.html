19.2

AI Product & UX Design

Building AI products people actually trust and use. This page is the functional, non-code layer — choosing what to build, designing for a model that is sometimes wrong, keeping a human in the loop, and earning trust through the interface — the difference between a clever model and a product that survives contact with users.

Section 01 BeginnerStart from zero

Assumed knowledge

None technical. This page is for anyone shaping an AI feature — engineer, PM, designer, founder. It sits above the implementation and decides what is worth implementing.

Most AI projects that fail do not fail on the model — they fail on the product. They automate the wrong thing, hide the model's uncertainty until it burns a user, or solve a problem nobody had. AI product thinking is the discipline of deciding what to build and how it should behave, given a component that is powerful, probabilistic and occasionally confidently wrong.

The one thing that makes AI products different

Traditional software is deterministic: the same input gives the same, correct output, so you design as if it always works. An AI feature is probabilistic and sometimes wrong — and that single fact changes everything about how you design it. You are not designing a feature that works; you are designing a feature that is right most of the time, and the product's quality is decided by how gracefully it handles being wrong. Every principle on this page follows from taking that seriously.

The words, in plain English
Human-in-the-loop
A person reviews or approves the AI's output before it takes effect.
Augmentation vs automation
Helping a person do a task vs replacing them on it.
Graceful failure
Being wrong in a safe, recoverable, honest way.
Calibrated trust
Users trusting the AI exactly as much as it deserves — no more, no less.
Feedback loop
Capturing whether the output was good, to improve the system.

Section 02 IntermediateChoosing what to build

Before any design, one question: is this a good problem for AI at all? The best AI features share a shape.

Is this a good fit for AI?
Good fit when…Poor fit when…
Mistakes are cheap and recoverableA single error is catastrophic or irreversible
"Roughly right" is genuinely usefulOnly exact, provable correctness will do
A human can easily verify the outputErrors are hard to notice until too late
The task is fuzzy, linguistic, or generativeA simple rule or formula would do it perfectly
Volume makes human-only impracticalLow volume where a human is cheaper and better
Two failure modes at the idea stage

Solving a non-problem: "add AI" is not a goal — a feature must solve a real user pain, and often a boring deterministic solution beats an AI one. If a regex, a lookup or a form would do the job reliably, use it. Over-automating: jumping straight to "the AI does it all" where the errors are expensive. The safer, and usually better, first version keeps a human in the loop and lets the AI assist. Earn the right to remove the human by proving the AI is good enough, measured (Topic 19.1) — do not assume it.

Section 03 IntermediateDesigning for a fallible model

Once you have a good problem, the design work is mostly about the model being wrong sometimes. A few principles carry most of the weight.

The core design principles
  1. Set expectationsTell users it is AI and can be wrong. Honesty upfront prevents betrayal later.
  2. Make verification easyShow sources, highlight what to check, keep the human's review cheap.
  3. Fail gracefully"I'm not sure" beats a confident wrong answer. Offer an exit and a fallback.
  4. Keep control reversibleDraft, don't send. Suggest, don't act. Let the user undo and edit.
Augment before you automate — the drafting pattern

The most reliable AI-product pattern is the draft: the AI proposes, the human disposes. It writes the email, the user edits and sends. It suggests the code, the developer reviews and commits. It drafts the summary, the analyst approves it. This keeps a human on the irreversible action (Topic 16.1), turns the model's occasional error into a caught typo rather than a shipped disaster, and — crucially — every human edit is feedback you can capture to improve the system (Topic 18.1). It is also the honest framing for users: the AI is a fast first draft, not an oracle. Automate fully only where you have measured that it is safe.

Section 04 AdvancedTrust, calibration & feedback

The subtle goal is calibrated trust — users relying on the AI exactly as much as it merits. Too little and they ignore a useful tool; too much and they get burned by a confident hallucination.

Designing for calibrated trust
LeverHow it calibrates trust
Citations & sourcesUsers can check claims; grounds trust in evidence (Topic 4.6)
Confidence signalsHedging on uncertain answers lowers trust appropriately
Showing the workReasoning or steps let users judge the output, not just accept it
Consistent honestyAdmitting "I don't know" once buys trust everywhere else
Easy correctionLetting users fix errors keeps them engaged rather than abandoning
Feedback is the product's second job

Every AI product should be quietly designed to learn from use. A thumbs up/down, an accepted vs rejected suggestion, an edited draft, a regenerated answer — each is a signal about quality that, captured, becomes evaluation data (Topic 19.1) and training data (Topic 18.1). The strongest AI products run a data flywheel: usage produces feedback, feedback improves the model, the better model attracts more usage. Designing the feedback capture into the interface from day one — not bolting it on later — is what turns a static feature into one that compounds. The interface is not just how users get value; it is how the product gets better.

Section 05 ProfessionalMeasuring an AI product

Traditional product metrics still apply, but AI products need two extra lenses. Quality in the world: not the offline eval score (Topic 19.1) but the real outcome — did the support issue get resolved, did the user accept the suggestion, did they have to redo the work? Trust & safety signals: escalation rate, correction rate, complaints, the frequency of the model being confidently wrong where it matters. A feature can have a great model and a failing product if users learn not to trust it, or if the one-in-a-hundred bad output is the one that ends up in front of a customer. Measure the product, not just the model — and treat cost and latency (Topic 19.3) as part of the user experience, because a brilliant answer that arrives in ten seconds or costs a fortune is often the wrong product.

Section 06 ProfessionalExercises

Do these — think like a product owner
  1. Take an AI feature idea and run it through the Section 02 fit test. Decide honestly whether AI is even the right tool.
  2. Redesign a feature that currently auto-acts into a draft/review flow. Describe what the human sees and approves.
  3. For a chatbot you use, find where it fails gracefully and where it fails badly. Redesign one bad failure.
  4. Design the feedback capture for a feature: what signal, how collected, how it feeds evaluation and training.
  5. Write the "this is AI and can be wrong" moment for a product without making it feel broken. Wording is the exercise.
  6. List the metrics you would put on a dashboard for an AI feature — product, quality-in-the-world, and trust/safety.
