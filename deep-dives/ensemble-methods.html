14.2

Ensemble Methods

Why many weak models beat one strong one. This page explains the three ways to combine models — bagging, boosting and stacking — the bias-variance intuition behind why they work, why gradient boosting is the quiet champion of tabular machine learning, and how the same "ensemble" instinct appears in self-consistency for LLMs.

Section 01 BeginnerStart from zero

Assumed knowledge

Decision trees and the bias-variance idea from classical ML (Topic 2.3).

One of the most reliable results in machine learning is almost too simple: combining many models usually beats any single one. A committee of imperfect predictors, aggregated well, outperforms the best individual predictor. This is ensembling, and it is behind most winning solutions on tabular data.

The wisdom-of-crowds intuition

Ask one person to guess the number of jellybeans in a jar and they will be off. Average a hundred guesses and the result is often remarkably close — individual errors cancel out. Ensembling applies this to models: each model makes different mistakes, so combining them cancels the errors and keeps the shared signal. The condition is that the models must be diverse — if they all make the same mistakes, averaging changes nothing. The whole art of ensembling is building models that are individually decent and collectively diverse.

The words, in plain English
Ensemble
A combination of several models used together.
Bagging
Train many models on random subsets of the data; average them.
Boosting
Train models in sequence, each fixing the previous ones' mistakes.
Stacking
Train a model to combine other models' predictions.
Weak learner
A model only slightly better than guessing — boosting's building block.
Diversity
Models making different errors — what makes an ensemble work.

The bias-variance framing (Topic 2.3) explains the split. Bagging trains many high-variance models (deep trees that overfit) on different data samples and averages them — averaging cancels the overfitting, cutting variance. That is a Random Forest. Boosting trains many high-bias models (shallow trees that underfit) in sequence, each correcting the last one's errors — stacking corrections reduces bias. That is gradient boosting. They attack opposite problems, which is why both help.

Section 03 AdvancedGradient boosting, the tabular champion

If you take one thing from this page: gradient boosting is, for tabular data, usually the model to beat. XGBoost, LightGBM and CatBoost win a large share of tabular competitions and power countless production systems.

How boosting works, in one idea

Train a simple model. Look at where it was wrong — the residual errors. Train a second model to predict those errors, and add it to the first. Now look at the remaining errors and train a third model on those. Each new model is a small correction, focused on what the ensemble still gets wrong. After hundreds of these tiny corrective steps, the sum is a highly accurate model built from weak ones. "Gradient" boosting formalises "predict the errors" as following the gradient of the loss (Topic 1.4) — each tree is a step downhill.

PythonGradient boosting in practice
from xgboost import XGBClassifier

model = XGBClassifier(
    n_estimators=500,        # up to 500 corrective trees...
    max_depth=4,             # ...each shallow (a weak learner)
    learning_rate=0.05,      # small steps -> more trees, better result
    subsample=0.8,           # row sampling adds diversity + regularisation
    colsample_bytree=0.8,    # feature sampling too
    early_stopping_rounds=50,# stop when the validation score stops improving
)
model.fit(X_train, y_train, eval_set=[(X_val, y_val)])

# It also hands you feature importances -- useful for the
# feature selection of Topic 14.1 and explainability (Topic 16.3).
importances = model.feature_importances_

Section 04 ProfessionalThe ensemble instinct beyond tabular ML

You have already met ensembling in the LLM world

The same "combine several attempts to cancel errors" idea appears throughout this handbook under other names. Self-consistency (Topic 3.1, 8.7) samples several LLM answers and takes the majority — a voting ensemble. Retrieval fusion (RRF, Topic 4.6) combines the rankings of several retrievers. Mixture-of-experts models route each token to different sub-networks. Even using a panel of LLM judges rather than one (Topic 4.7) is ensembling. Recognising the pattern — diverse components, aggregated to cancel individual errors — lets you apply a proven idea in new places, and reminds you that many "new" AI techniques are old statistical wisdom in modern dress.

Ensembling is not free

More models means more compute, more latency, and more to maintain and explain. A single well-tuned gradient-boosting model is often the right stopping point — the further gains from heavy stacking are frequently tiny and not worth the operational cost outside a competition. The same applies to LLM self-consistency: sampling five answers costs five times as much, so reserve it for high-stakes decisions. Ensemble when the accuracy genuinely matters more than the cost; otherwise, one good model wins on total value.

Section 05 ProfessionalExercises

Do these — roughly an evening
  1. On a tabular dataset, compare a single decision tree, a random forest (bagging) and XGBoost (boosting). Rank them.
  2. Tune XGBoost's learning_rate and n_estimators together. Observe the trade-off (smaller rate needs more trees).
  3. Build a simple stacking ensemble combining a tree model, a linear model and a k-NN. Beat the best individual.
  4. Deliberately make your ensemble members identical. Confirm the ensemble no longer helps — diversity is essential.
  5. Compare LightGBM's speed against XGBoost on a large dataset.
  6. Implement self-consistency for an LLM reasoning task (Topic 3.1). Note it is the same idea as a voting ensemble.
