Speech & Audio AI

Machines that hear and speak. This page covers speech recognition (Whisper), text-to-speech, and the voice-agent loop that stitches them together with an LLM, how audio becomes something a model can process, the latency problem that defines real-time voice, and where audio AI fits a production system.

Section 01 BeginnerStart from zero

Assumed knowledge

How models process sequences (Topics 1.3, 12.2) and multimodal basics (Topic 6.2). Audio is another modality feeding the same kinds of model.

Audio AI covers two directions and a loop that joins them: turning speech into text (recognition), turning text into speech (synthesis), and combining both with an LLM to build a voice agent you can talk to. Voice interfaces — assistants, call-centre automation, transcription, accessibility — are a large and growing use of AI.

How audio becomes model-friendly

Sound is a waveform — air pressure over time, sampled thousands of times a second. Feeding raw samples to a model is unwieldy, so audio is usually converted to a spectrogram: a picture of which frequencies are present over time. That turns audio into an image-like grid, which the same architectures that handle images and sequences (CNNs, transformers) can process (Topics 12.1, 1.3). So audio AI is not a separate universe — it is the familiar architectures pointed at spectrograms. Whisper, the leading speech recogniser, is a transformer that reads spectrograms and writes text.

Section 03 AdvancedThe voice-agent loop, and why latency rules

A voice assistant chains the pieces: hear, understand, respond, speak. The classic architecture is three models in a loop.

  1. STT transcribes what the user said.
  2. LLM (possibly an agent, Phase 08) decides the response.
  3. TTS speaks the response aloud.
  4. Repeat — a spoken conversation.
Latency is the defining challenge of voice

In a spoken conversation, humans expect a reply in a few hundred milliseconds. A pause of even one second feels broken. But the classic loop has three sequential model calls — transcribe, think, synthesise — each adding delay, and the LLM step is the slow one (Topic 1.3). Making voice feel natural is largely a latency engineering problem: stream everything (start transcribing before the user finishes, start speaking before the LLM finishes, Topic 1.3); detect when the user has stopped talking quickly; and increasingly, use integrated speech-to-speech models that skip the text round-trip entirely, cutting the delay dramatically. If you build voice, budget your latency like money — every stage counts, and streaming is not optional.

The direction of travel is away from three-model pipelines toward single models that take audio in and produce audio out, reasoning natively over speech. These are faster (no text bottleneck) and can preserve tone and emotion that text discards. For now, both architectures are in use; the pipeline gives you control and model choice, the integrated model gives you latency and naturalness. Either way, the LLM skills from the rest of this handbook — prompting, RAG, agents, tools — apply directly; voice is an interface layer over them.

Voice cloning is a serious risk

Modern TTS can clone a specific person's voice from seconds of audio — enabling scams (fake calls from a "family member" or "CEO"), fraud and misinformation. If you build with voice synthesis, treat consent and provenance as first-class concerns (Topic 6.4): synthesise only voices you have the right to, disclose synthetic audio, and be aware your transcription and voice systems handle sensitive personal data (recordings are biometric and often confidential, Topic 6.4). The same technology that makes an accessible narrator makes a convincing scam — the responsibility is part of building it.

Section 05 ProfessionalExercises

Do these — roughly a weekend
  1. Transcribe an audio file with Whisper locally. Try a noisy clip and a non-English one — note its robustness.
  2. Visualise a spectrogram of a short clip. See how audio becomes an image the model reads (Section 01).
  3. Build a simple voice-agent loop: STT → LLM → TTS. Measure end-to-end latency at each stage.
  4. Add streaming to the loop — start responding before the LLM finishes. Measure the perceived-latency improvement.
  5. Feed meeting transcripts into a RAG system (Phase 4) and ask questions about them.
  6. Read your provider's voice-cloning and consent policy. Write a paragraph on the safeguards you would put in a voice product.
