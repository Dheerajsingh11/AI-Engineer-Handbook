A Short History of AI

Why the field looks the way it does. A short, useful history of AI — symbolic systems, the winters, the machine-learning turn, the deep-learning revolution, the transformer, and the generative moment — so you understand not just what today's tools are but why they exist and what cycles they are part of.

Section 01 BeginnerStart from zero

Assumed knowledge

The nesting of AI / ML / deep learning / generative AI from Topic 11.1. History is more meaningful once you know the categories it produced.

You do not need history to use today's tools — but you understand them far better for it. The field has moved in waves: bursts of optimism, then disappointment, then a new idea that changes everything. Knowing the pattern helps you read the present with the right mix of excitement and skepticism, and it explains why certain approaches dominate now.

The one lesson from the whole history

Again and again, the approaches that won were the ones that learned from data and scaled with compute, beating hand-crafted rules built by experts. This is sometimes called "the bitter lesson": general methods that leverage computation eventually overtake clever human-designed ones. Every wave below is a version of that lesson being re-learned. It is the single most useful thing to carry out of this page, because it still predicts where the field is going.

Section 03 IntermediateSymbolic AI and why it hit a wall

The first few decades bet on symbolic AI: intelligence as manipulating symbols by explicit rules. Expert systems encoded a specialist's knowledge as thousands of hand-written if…then rules — a medical diagnosis system, a chemistry analyser. For narrow, well-defined domains, they worked and were commercially valuable.

Why it stalled. The real world is too messy for hand-written rules. Every exception needs another rule; the rules interact in unforeseen ways; and for perception — recognising a face, understanding a sentence — nobody can write the rules at all, because we cannot articulate how we do it ourselves. Expert systems became unmaintainable as they grew, and the gap between the hype and what they delivered triggered the AI winters: two periods where disappointment collapsed funding and interest. The lesson, learned painfully: you cannot program your way to intelligence one rule at a time.

Section 04 IntermediateThe turn to learning, and the deep-learning breakthrough

The escape was to stop writing rules and start learning them from data (Topic 11.1). Statistical machine learning grew through the 1990s and 2000s — spam filters, recommendation systems, search ranking — quietly becoming the workhorse of the internet. Then, in 2012, deep learning broke through.

  1. 2012 — AlexNet. A deep neural network crushed the ImageNet image-recognition benchmark, far ahead of hand-engineered methods. The proof that deep networks, given enough data and GPU compute, dominate.
  2. 2012–2017 — deep learning everywhere. Speech recognition, translation, image understanding, game-playing (AlphaGo, 2016) all fell to deep networks.
  3. Three ingredients made it possible: big labelled datasets (the internet), parallel compute (GPUs, repurposed from graphics), and better training techniques. None was enough alone.
Why GPUs, of all things

A neural network is mostly matrix multiplication (Topic 1.1), and graphics cards were built to do exactly that, in parallel, for rendering. Repurposing GPUs for training gave a hundredfold speed-up and made deep learning practical. It is a recurring shape in this history: a breakthrough waits for the hardware to catch up to the idea. The idea of neural networks was decades old; it only worked once the compute arrived. That relationship — algorithms gated by hardware — still governs the field, which is why GPU supply is a strategic concern today.

Section 05 AdvancedThe transformer and the age of scale

In 2017 a paper titled Attention Is All You Need introduced the transformer (Topic 1.3) — the architecture behind every large language model today. Its importance was not just quality; it was that it scaled: make it bigger, feed it more data, give it more compute, and it kept getting better, more reliably than anything before.

The scaling era, in milestones
YearWhat happenedWhy it mattered
2017The transformerAn architecture that scales gracefully
2018–2020BERT, GPT-2, GPT-3Bigger models, surprising new abilities
2022ChatGPTGenerative AI reaches everyone, via chat
2023–nowMultimodal, tools, agents, reasoningFrom chatbot to systems that act (Phase 08)

Emergence and the scaling hypothesis. The startling discovery of this era was that abilities emerged from scale that nobody explicitly built in: reasoning steps, translation, code, in-context learning. Scaling up a next-word predictor produced qualitatively new behaviour. This drove the "scaling hypothesis" — that much of the path to more capable AI is simply more scale — which is why the frontier has been a race in model and compute size. Whether scale alone continues to deliver, or new ideas are needed, is the central open question of the field right now.

Section 06 ProfessionalReading the present with history in mind

Hype cycles are the norm, not the exception

The field has been through booms and busts before, and the current moment has real substance and real hype tangled together. History suggests two habits. First, separate demos from deployed reality — every wave produced dazzling demos, and the ones that mattered were the ones that shipped and held up. Second, watch what scales and what does not — the winning approaches keep being the general, data-and-compute-driven ones. When you evaluate a new AI claim, ask: is this the bitter lesson working again, or clever hand-engineering that will not generalise? That question, drawn straight from this history, is a reliable filter.

Section 07 ProfessionalExercises

Do these — roughly an hour
  1. Draw the timeline from memory: symbolic AI → winters → ML turn → deep learning → transformer → generative. Explain what broke and what broke through at each step.
  2. Explain "the bitter lesson" in one sentence and give one example of it from the history above.
  3. Find a current AI product and identify which era's idea it is built on (rules, statistical ML, deep learning, or generative).
  4. Read about one AI winter. What was over-promised, and what does that suggest about a current claim you have seen?
  5. Argue both sides of the scaling hypothesis in a paragraph each: will more scale keep working, or is a new idea needed?
