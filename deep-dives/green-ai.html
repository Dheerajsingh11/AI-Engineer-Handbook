Green AI & Compute Cost

The energy bill behind the intelligence. This page covers Green AI — the compute, energy and carbon cost of training and running models — where that cost actually goes, how to measure it, and the levers that cut it (which, conveniently, are the same ones that cut your cloud bill).

Section 01 BeginnerStart from zero

Assumed knowledge

AI hardware (Topic 6.5) and efficiency engineering (Topic 19.3). This page reframes the same compute through the lens of energy and carbon.

Every token a model generates and every step it trains burns electricity, and electricity has a cost — in money, and in carbon. As models and usage have grown, so has that footprint. Green AI is the practice of being aware of, measuring, and reducing the energy and carbon cost of AI — not as a moral add-on, but because the same efficiency that saves the planet a little also saves you a lot of money.

Efficiency is the rare win-win

The reason to care even if sustainability is not your top concern: cost, latency, and carbon are the same axis. A smaller, quantised, well-cached model uses less energy, costs less to run, responds faster, and emits less carbon — all at once. So Green AI is not a trade-off against performance; it is mostly good engineering (Topics 19.3, 18.3) described in a different unit. The teams that take it seriously tend to have cheaper, faster products as a side effect.

The words, in plain English
FLOPs
Floating-point operations — a rough proxy for how much compute a task needs.
kWh
Kilowatt-hours — the actual energy used.
CO₂e
Carbon-dioxide equivalent — energy converted to emissions using the grid's carbon intensity.
Carbon intensity
How dirty the electricity is — varies a lot by region and time of day.
Embodied carbon
The emissions from manufacturing the hardware, not just running it.

Section 02 IntermediateWhere the energy goes

The cost splits into two very different phases, and knowing which dominates decides where to spend your effort.

Training vs inference, energetically
TrainingInference
WhenOnce (or occasionally)Every request, forever
Cost shapeA large, one-off spikeA small cost paid billions of times
VisibilityHeadline-grabbing ("training cost X")Quiet, but usually the bigger lifetime total
Your leverTrain less; reuse/fine-tune instead of from scratchServe efficiently (the biggest win for most teams)

The counter-intuitive point, same as the cost story (Topic 6.5): for most organisations, inference dominates the lifetime footprint, not training. A model trained once but served to millions of users for years spends far more total energy answering than it ever did learning. So unless you are a frontier lab training giant models, the highest-leverage Green-AI work is making inference efficient — which is exactly the efficiency engineering of Topic 19.3. The greenest training run, meanwhile, is usually the one you don't do: fine-tune or reuse an existing model instead of pre-training from scratch (Topic 9.5).

Section 03 IntermediateMeasuring it

You cannot reduce what you do not measure (the theme of Topic 19.1, applied to energy). The chain from compute to carbon is straightforward.

From energy to carbon CO₂e  =  energy (kWh)  ×  carbon intensity (kg CO₂e / kWh) Energy used depends on the hardware's power draw × time × a data-centre overhead factor (PUE). Carbon intensity depends on where and when you run — the same job can emit several times more on a coal-heavy grid than on a hydro/nuclear/solar one. Tools like CodeCarbon estimate this automatically from your hardware and region.
The region and timing lever

One of the easiest wins requires no model change at all: run compute where and when the grid is clean. Carbon intensity varies enormously by region and even by hour (more solar at midday, more coal at night). Scheduling training jobs in low-carbon regions, or at low-carbon times, can cut emissions by a large factor for the same work — "carbon-aware scheduling." For an engineer this is just a data-centre-region choice and a scheduler, and cloud providers increasingly expose the carbon data to make it.

"Right-size the model" is the master lever

Every lever above is a specific case of one principle you have met all through Phases 18–19: use the smallest model that clears the quality bar, served as efficiently as possible. The instinct to reach for the biggest frontier model for every task is the single largest source of wasted energy (and money) in applied AI. A distilled, quantised model that meets the requirement on cheap hardware, answering from a cache when it can, is the green choice and the economical choice at once. Green AI, in the end, is not a separate discipline — it is efficiency engineering with the externality made visible.

Section 05 ProfessionalBeing honest about it

Two professional cautions. First, avoid greenwashing: report real numbers (kWh, CO₂e, region) rather than vague "eco-friendly" claims, and include the honest caveats — estimates are rough, and embodied carbon (manufacturing the GPUs) is real too. Second, keep perspective: for a typical application team, your footprint is dominated by inference volume, so the responsible move is simply to right-size and serve efficiently, and to measure so you can show it. The field also has a healthy counter-current — "Green AI" as a research value that rewards efficiency and reporting compute, not just chasing accuracy at any cost. As an engineer, you contribute most by defaulting to the efficient option and being transparent about what your systems actually cost to run — which, happily, is the same habit that keeps the cloud bill down (Topic 19.3).

Section 06 ProfessionalExercises

Do these — put a number on it
  1. Wrap a training run in CodeCarbon. Record the kWh and estimated CO₂e. Now you have a baseline.
  2. Quantise a model and re-measure the energy per 1,000 inferences. Report the % reduction in energy and cost.
  3. Estimate a feature's lifetime footprint: training once vs inference over a year at your expected traffic. See which dominates.
  4. Compare the carbon intensity of two cloud regions and estimate the emissions saved by moving a job to the cleaner one.
  5. Take a task currently on a frontier model and find the smallest model that still passes your eval (Topic 19.1). Quantify the energy saved.
  6. Write the honest sustainability note for a project: real numbers, the region, and the caveats (estimates, embodied carbon).
