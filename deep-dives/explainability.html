16.3

Explainability (XAI)

Opening the black box. This page covers explainable AI — why understanding a model's decisions matters, the methods for tabular models (feature importance, SHAP, LIME) and for LLMs (attention, citations, chain-of-thought), the crucial caveat that a model's stated reasoning may not be its real reasoning, and how to build systems whose decisions you can actually defend.

Section 01 BeginnerStart from zero

Assumed knowledge

Classical ML (Topic 2.3), how LLMs work (Topic 1.3), and responsible AI (Topic 16.1). Explainability serves fairness, debugging and trust.

Machine-learning models, especially deep ones, are often black boxes: they produce accurate predictions but do not tell you why. Explainable AI (XAI) is the set of techniques for understanding and communicating a model's decisions. It matters for four concrete reasons, not abstract virtue.

Why explainability matters
ReasonConcretely
TrustPeople act on decisions they understand; reject ones they cannot
DebuggingYou cannot fix a wrong prediction you cannot explain
FairnessExplanations reveal bias — is it using a proxy? (Topic 16.1)
ComplianceSome decisions legally require an explanation (Topic 6.4)
The accuracy-interpretability trade-off

There is often a tension: the most accurate models (deep networks, large ensembles) are the least interpretable, and the most interpretable (linear models, small decision trees) are sometimes less accurate. XAI tries to get the best of both — keep the accurate model, add tools that explain it. But note the deeper point: for high-stakes decisions, a slightly less accurate model you can fully explain may be the better engineering choice than a black box you cannot. A loan-denial you cannot justify is a legal and ethical liability regardless of its accuracy. Interpretability is a feature, sometimes worth trading a little accuracy for.

The words, in plain English
Interpretable
You can understand how the model decides, by its nature.
Explainable
You can produce an explanation for a decision, even from a black box.
Feature importance
Which inputs mattered most to the model overall.
Attribution
How much each input contributed to this specific prediction.
SHAP / LIME
Popular methods for per-prediction attribution.
Faithfulness
Whether an explanation reflects what the model actually did.

Section 02 IntermediateExplaining tabular models

XAI methods for classic ML
MethodAnswersScope
Coefficients (linear models)Each feature's direction and weightGlobal, built-in
Feature importance (trees)Which features the model relies onGlobal
SHAPEach feature's contribution to this predictionLocal + global; theoretically grounded
LIMEA simple local model approximating this predictionLocal
Partial dependenceHow a feature affects predictions on averageGlobal
PythonExplaining a prediction with SHAP
import shap

# SHAP attributes a prediction to its features, with a solid
# theoretical basis (from game theory -- fair credit assignment).
explainer = shap.TreeExplainer(model)          # works great with gradient boosting
shap_values = explainer.shap_values(X)

# For ONE prediction: why did the model decide this?
shap.plots.waterfall(shap_values[0])
# e.g. "denied because: high debt-ratio (+0.3), short history (+0.2),
#       offset by: high income (-0.15)". A defensible explanation.

# Across all predictions: what drives the model overall?
shap.plots.beeswarm(shap_values)

# This is also how you CATCH bias (Topic 16.1): if a proxy for a
# protected attribute shows high SHAP importance, investigate.

SHAP is the practical workhorse. It gives both per-prediction explanations ("why was this loan denied?") and global insight ("what drives the model in general?"), it is grounded in a fair-attribution principle from game theory, and it doubles as a bias-detection and debugging tool (Topic 16.1). For tabular models — the ones where explanation is most often legally and operationally required — SHAP on a gradient-boosting model (Topic 14.2) is a strong, defensible default.

Section 03 AdvancedExplaining LLMs, and a crucial caveat

LLMs are far harder to explain — billions of parameters, no clean feature attributions. The practical "explanations" you have are different in kind.

How LLM decisions are made legible
MethodGives you
Citations (RAG)Which sources the answer drew on — the most practical "explanation" (Phase 4)
Chain-of-thoughtThe model's stated reasoning steps (Topic 3.1)
Attention visualisationWhich input tokens the model attended to (Topic 1.3)
Token log-probs / entropyHow confident the model was (Topic 1.2)
Mechanistic interpretabilityReverse-engineering internal circuits — research frontier
A model's stated reasoning may not be its real reasoning

This is the most important caveat in the whole topic. When an LLM produces a chain-of-thought (Topic 3.1) or "explains" its answer, that explanation is generated text, not a faithful readout of the computation that produced the answer. Models can reach the right answer with faulty stated reasoning, or the wrong answer with plausible-sounding reasoning; the explanation is a post-hoc story, not a mechanism trace. The same caution applies to attention weights — "the model attended to X" does not reliably mean "X caused the output." So do not treat an LLM's self-explanation as an audit trail you can present to a regulator or fully trust for debugging. For genuine accountability, rely on things that are actually verifiable: the retrieved sources (citations), the inputs, the tool calls, and external checks — not the model's narration of itself.

Section 04 ProfessionalBuilding explainable systems

Design for explainability, don't bolt it on

The most defensible explanations often come from system design, not from prying open a model. Prefer architectures that are transparent by construction: RAG with mandatory citations (Phase 4) means every claim points to a source you can check — a far stronger "explanation" than any attention plot. Routing and tools (Topics 8.2, 8.4) create an auditable trace of what happened. Structured decision records (Topic 6.4) capture the inputs, model version, retrieved context and any human review. An interpretable model where the stakes demand it (Section 01) may beat a black box. The pattern: build systems whose decisions are inherently traceable, rather than deploying an opaque model and hoping post-hoc XAI can reconstruct what it did. Verifiable structure beats plausible narration every time.

Explainability by system type
SystemExplanation strategy
Tabular decision (loan, risk)Interpretable model or SHAP on gradient boosting
RAG Q&AMandatory citations to sources (Phase 4)
Agent actionThe trajectory trace: reasoning, tools, results (Topic 8.10)
High-stakes, regulatedDecision records + human review + interpretable where possible (Topic 6.4)

Section 05 ProfessionalExercises

Do these — roughly a weekend
  1. Train a gradient-boosting model and use SHAP to explain individual predictions. Produce a plain-language reason for one decision.
  2. Use SHAP global importance to check whether a proxy for a protected attribute is driving the model (Topic 16.1).
  3. Compare an interpretable model (logistic regression) and a black box on the same task. Weigh the accuracy-explainability trade-off (Section 01).
  4. Build a RAG system with mandatory citations. Argue why this is a stronger "explanation" than an attention plot.
  5. Get an LLM to give a confident answer with faulty stated reasoning — demonstrate the Section 03 caveat.
  6. Design a decision record for a high-stakes AI decision that would satisfy an auditor (Topic 6.4).
