Image, Video & Music Generation

Generating images, video and music — the applied side. This page is about using generative media models rather than their internals (which live in Topics 12.4-12.5): how to prompt and control image generation, the state of video and music, the production concerns, and the provenance and rights questions you cannot ignore.

Section 01 BeginnerStart from zero

Assumed knowledge

Diffusion models (Topic 12.5) for how image generation works internally. This page is the applied counterpart — using these models in a product.

You have seen how image generators work under the hood (diffusion, Topic 12.5). This page is about using them: the practical craft of getting good output, controlling it, and shipping it responsibly. Generative media — images, video, music, 3D — is a fast-moving space with real commercial uses in marketing, design, entertainment and prototyping.

The skill is control, not just prompting

Anyone can type a prompt and get an image. The engineering value is control and consistency: getting a specific composition, matching a brand style, keeping a character consistent across images, iterating reliably. A one-off pretty picture is easy; a repeatable pipeline that produces on-brand, on-spec media at scale is the actual product. That is where the control techniques below — and the same measure-and-iterate discipline as the rest of this handbook — earn their keep.

ControlNet is the professional's tool. Text alone cannot specify "this exact pose" or "this precise layout." ControlNet conditions the diffusion process (Topic 12.5) on a structural input — a stick-figure pose, an edge map, a depth map — so you control composition precisely while the model fills in style and detail. Combined with a LoRA fine-tune (the same technique as Topic 3.5, applied to an image model) that teaches a consistent character or brand style, you can produce controlled, on-brand images repeatably. This composition — ControlNet for structure, LoRA for style, prompt for content — is how serious image pipelines are built.

Section 03 AdvancedVideo and music

Video generation extends the diffusion idea (Topic 12.5) across time — generating frames that are consistent with each other and move coherently, which is much harder than a single image. It has moved from unusable to genuinely usable for short clips remarkably fast, and is a very active frontier. The current reality: strong for short, stylised clips; still limited on length, fine control, and perfect consistency. Costs and generation times are high. Treat it as promising and improving rather than a solved production tool — but track it, because it is moving quickly.

Music generation produces original audio from a text prompt ("upbeat corporate background, 90 seconds"). It is good enough for background tracks, sound design and prototyping, and it raises the same rights questions as images (what was it trained on, who owns the output). For production use, licensing clarity matters as much as quality.

Rights, provenance and consent are not optional

Generative media sits on genuinely unsettled legal and ethical ground, and shipping it without addressing this is a real risk:

  • Training data. Many models were trained on copyrighted images or music without clear licensing; the legal status is being litigated. Prefer models with clear commercial-use terms.
  • Output ownership. Who owns AI-generated media, and can it be copyrighted, varies by jurisdiction and is changing.
  • Deepfakes & likeness. Generating a real person's face or voice without consent is harmful and, increasingly, illegal (Topics 12.4, 6.4).
  • Provenance. Disclosing that media is AI-generated — and watermarking it (e.g. C2PA content credentials) — is becoming an expectation and, in places, a requirement.

If you build with generative media, put licence terms, consent, safety filtering and provenance into the design from the start — the same responsible-AI discipline as Topic 6.4, applied to pixels and audio.

Section 05 ProfessionalExercises

Do these — roughly a weekend
  1. Generate images with a Stable Diffusion pipeline. Systematically vary prompt, guidance scale and seed; document what each does.
  2. Use ControlNet to control composition from a pose or edge map. Note the control text alone cannot give you.
  3. Use img2img and inpainting to edit an existing image rather than generate from scratch.
  4. Fine-tune a LoRA (Topic 3.5) to reproduce a consistent style or object across generations.
  5. Build a small pipeline: generate several candidates, auto-filter, return the best — a repeatable product, not a one-off.
  6. Check the licence and provenance policy of a model you would use commercially. Write your rights-and-consent checklist.
